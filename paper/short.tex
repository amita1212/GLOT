\begin{abstract}
Hyperbolic space represents tree-like structure with less distortion than flat
space, and transformer token states are often described as tree-like. That
pairing has motivated hyperbolic versions of many models. We ask whether it
actually helps, on one architecture, under controlled conditions.

Our subject is \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot}, a sentence pooler that
builds a graph over the tokens of a frozen language model, refines it with a
GNN, and aggregates it into a single vector. Geometry enters in exactly three
places --- how the graph is built (\textbf{Stage A}), how states are pooled
(\textbf{Stage B}), how messages combine (\textbf{Stage C}) --- so we replace
each independently and give all eight combinations the same search budget over
fifteen shared seeds.

\textbf{The three stages disagree, and that is the main result.} Stage B, the
hyperbolic readout, is reliably harmful --- on two encoders and a decoder, with
the effect size replicating to within $0.2$ Spearman across backbones --- and we
can say why: it amplifies seed variance rather than acting as a useful prior.
Stage C, hyperbolic message passing, is the stage that helps, and its end-to-end
effect is significant. Stage A, the hyperbolic graph, is positive on CoLA at
fifty seeds but separates from the baseline nowhere else. What does \emph{not}
transfer is how the stages combine: the sign of the B$\times$C interaction
changes with task and backbone.

\textbf{We are careful about the one gain, and the powered run made us more
careful, not less.} Stage C is worth $+1.69$ MCC on CoLA over $65$ seeds
($t=5.91$, $55/10$), but a factorial that separates curvature from the graph
density and architecture it was tuned alongside cannot attribute it to either:
the largest single component is the \emph{configuration} ($+0.88$), not the
curvature ($+0.53$), and neither is significant on both tests. The
B$\times$C-style interaction we sized the run to detect is $+0.27$ and absent ---
the $+1.36$ we had estimated from $15$ seeds was noise. Separately, the released
training code does not pin a run to its seed: re-running the identical
configuration at the identical seed moves CoLA by up to $3.6$ MCC, larger than
the effect.

\textbf{We also question the premise.} The usual justification for going
hyperbolic is a low measured $\delta$-hyperbolicity. That statistic is an
artifact of a few very large token norms: on an outlier-free metric the
backbones it is used to tell apart are indistinguishable ($0.2127$ against
$0.2114$), while the Euclidean version differs between them by two orders of
magnitude. Measured tree-likeness cannot tell you where curvature will help ---
only a controlled, component-wise comparison can.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Turning a language model's token states into one vector is a first step for
most sentence tasks. Simple poolers --- mean, max, \texttt{[CLS]} --- treat
tokens as an unordered bag. A newer idea is to treat pooling as \emph{relations
first, aggregation second}: build a graph over tokens, refine it with a GNN,
then aggregate. \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot} is a strong example,
matching competitive GLUE and MTEB numbers with $20\times$ fewer trained
parameters and a frozen backbone. Our runs support the claim it rests on: on
the authors' own noise test, \textsc{Glot} beats every bag-of-tokens pooler by
$14$--$35$ points at every distractor ratio, in every seed
(Appendix~\ref{app:stress}).

This paper asks what else that design can carry. Hyperbolic space is a natural
candidate for all three of its parts, so we build a hyperbolic version of each
and measure them separately. That separation is the point: two of the three
move in opposite directions, so a single ``hyperbolic'' switch would have
reported their average and found nothing.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{A stage-wise hyperbolic study} (\S\ref{sec:results}). Under an
      equal search budget with $15$ shared seeds, Stage B is significantly
      harmful on two encoders and on a decoder; Stage C is significantly
      positive end to end on CoLA; Stage A is positive on CoLA once given $50$
      seeds and separates from the baseline nowhere else. The individual stages
      replicate across backbones; the interaction between them does not.
\item \textbf{A seed is not a run} (\S\ref{sec:choices},
      Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}). The released code sets the Python,
      NumPy and Torch RNGs but never requests deterministic kernels. Re-running
      one configuration at one seed moves CoLA MCC by up to $3.61$, with paired
      $\mathrm{sd}=1.47$ --- larger than the effects this paper reports.
      This is not a caveat we inherited from elsewhere; it changed one of our
      own numbers by a factor of six.
\item \textbf{The usual motivation does not survive measurement}
      (\S\ref{sec:premise}). Low $\delta$-hyperbolicity is driven by a handful
      of very large token norms. On an outlier-free metric the backbones it is
      used to tell apart are indistinguishable.
\item \textbf{The choices that changed our answers} (\S\ref{sec:choices}). A
      cache-warming order worth $5$ MCC, a search that reverses arm rankings, a
      threshold that silently produces a complete graph on RoBERTa, and four
      defects in the released code.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What we changed}
\label{sec:method}

Let $x_1,\dots,x_L$ be the token states of a frozen backbone. \textsc{Glot}
joins two tokens when their cosine passes a threshold,
\begin{equation}
A_{ij} = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, \cos(x_i, x_j) > \tau \,\right],
\label{eq:adj}
\end{equation}
runs $K$ graph-attention layers over the result, and aggregates with a learned
readout. Only the pooler trains. Geometry enters in three places, so we replace
each in turn. Appendix~\ref{app:math} gives the Poincar\'e ball, the maps
$\exp^c_0$ and $\log^c_0$, and the geodesic distance we use.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} in Appendix~\ref{app:fig} shows where each stage sits.

\paragraph{Stage A --- a Poincar\'e graph.} Lift the tokens into the ball and
join them when their geodesic distance falls below the $q$-quantile of all
pairwise distances,
$A_{ij} = \mathbf{1}[\,d_c(\exp^c_0(\tilde{x}_i), \exp^c_0(\tilde{x}_j)) < \rho_q\,]$.
Using a quantile rather than a fixed radius fixes the edge count at $q$, so A
and the cosine baseline can be compared at the same sparsity.

\paragraph{Stage B --- an Einstein gyro-midpoint readout.} Replace the weighted
sum with the Einstein midpoint in Klein coordinates,
\begin{equation}
m_K = \frac{\sum_i a_i \gamma_{k_i} k_i}{\sum_i a_i \gamma_{k_i}},
\qquad k = \frac{2x}{1 + c\|x\|^2},
\qquad \gamma_k = (1 - c\|k\|^2)^{-1/2},
\label{eq:midpoint}
\end{equation}
then map back and into the tangent space for the task head. Unlike a plain mean
this depends on curvature: as $c \rightarrow 0$ it becomes the arithmetic mean.

\paragraph{Stage C --- M\"obius message passing.} Replace each GNN layer with a
hyperbolic one that lifts to the tangent space to combine neighbours,
\begin{equation}
h_i' = \exp^c_0\!\Big(\sigma\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)}
       \alpha_{ij}\, \log^c_0\big(W \otimes_c h_j\big)\Big)\Big),
\end{equation}
with $W \otimes_c h = \exp^c_0(W \log^c_0(h))$ and $\alpha_{ij}$ either
degree-based (HGCN) or attention (HGAT).

\paragraph{Curvature is searched in a scale-free way.} Curvature and feature
scale cannot be told apart here. The geodesic distance depends only on
$\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|$, so doubling the features and quartering $c$ leaves every
distance unchanged, and searching $c$ on its own wastes most of the grid. We
instead rescale features to unit mean norm and search the \emph{effective
radius} $r_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{c}\,\mathbb{E}\|x\|$ over
$\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3\}$. This is not cosmetic: with raw BERT token norms
($\approx 14.7$) the map $\exp^c_0$ saturates to radius $1.0$ in
\texttt{float32}, real pairwise distances land in $[8.85, 11.76]$, and any
fixed distance threshold below about $9$ returns an empty graph
(Appendix~\ref{app:defects}).

We run all eight combinations of A, B and C, plus a \texttt{no graph} control
that deletes the token graph but keeps the trained projection and readout, and
on RoBERTa two threshold-calibration arms (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}).

\section{How we ran it, and the choices that changed the answer}
\label{sec:choices}

Every arm is frozen-backbone, $2$ epochs, Adam, batch $32$; only the pooler
trains. Tuning draws $40$ configurations at one held-out seed ($10$ on the
decoder, where a run costs four times as much). Confirmation re-runs the winner
on the fixed seed set $\{1,\dots,15\}$, shared across arms. We report the exact
two-sided sign test beside the paired $t$-test and call something significant
only when both agree, with a Bonferroni correction across the arms tested
against a common baseline within a campaign. Full protocol in
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}. Six choices moved our results more than the
effects we were trying to measure.

\paragraph{A seed does not pin a run, and this is the one that hurt.} The code
seeds Python, NumPy and Torch, but never sets
\texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms} or the cuDNN determinism flags,
and the scatter reductions underneath message passing are non-deterministic on
CUDA. We found this by accident: the extended factorial re-ran the CoLA baseline
cell under a configuration string \emph{identical} to the one already in the
main campaign, and disagreed on all $15$ seeds --- mean $+0.334$ MCC,
$\mathrm{sd}\ 1.472$, largest single-seed gap $3.61$
(Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}). Two consequences. First, a table that splices
a cell from one campaign into another is unsafe, and our own published
decomposition of Stage C did exactly that (\S\ref{sec:stageC}). Second, pairing
buys less than we claimed: on STS-B it still halves the variance, but on CoLA
the paired difference is \emph{noisier} than the independent one, so the seed
explains almost nothing there. All within-campaign comparisons in this paper are
unaffected --- both arms of every paired test were run in the same campaign, and
re-run non-determinism sits inside the paired scatter the tests already estimate
--- but we would not have known that without checking.

\paragraph{The search space must be the same for every arm.} An earlier
campaign of ours searched only graph knobs and left the learning rate, depth and
width pinned at values picked for a \emph{flat} pooler. That handed the baseline
an advantage and produced a false positive we retract in
Appendix~\ref{app:narrow}: Stage A on STS-B looked like $+0.223$ Spearman with
$15/15$ seeds and $t(14)=6.20$, and collapsed to $+0.028$ once the baseline was
allowed to pick $K=4$. Every BERT and RoBERTa campaign here uses \textsc{Glot}'s
full grid applied identically to all arms: $40$ trials each, $15$ seeds, $495$
runs per task on BERT and $1{,}980$ across the four GLUE tasks. What has to be
equal is the budget \emph{within} a campaign, not the same number everywhere ---
the decoder gave every arm $10$ trials rather than $40$, for cost.

\paragraph{The search really does use that freedom.} The learning rate does not
settle on one value. On STS-B all nine arms chose $2\times10^{-4}$; on CoLA
eight of nine chose $1\times10^{-3}$; MRPC and RTE split. Depth, width and
threshold move too. Arms were therefore not locked to a rate chosen for a flat
pooler, which is the main form of the objection that non-Euclidean layers get
judged under flat-tuned optimisers. Appendix~\ref{app:hp} lists the selected
configurations.

\paragraph{Tuning maxima are inflated, and can reverse a ranking.} With $T$
trials and per-seed noise $\sigma$, the best of $T$ sits above the truth even
for an arm identical to the baseline --- about $1.83$ here on STS-B. On the
RoBERTa campaign the inflation is large enough to flip the order outright: the
quantile correction is first of five at the tuning maximum ($56.77$) and last
of five over $15$ seeds ($53.19$). Reporting tuning maxima would have inverted
our conclusion. We report confirmation means only.

\paragraph{Cache order is worth about $5$ MCC.} The released code caches
backbone states and builds the shuffled loader \emph{before} the classifier is
created. On a cache miss the loader consumes the global RNG; on a hit it does
not. The same seed therefore gives a different initialisation depending on cache
state --- $40.37$ cold against $45.54$ warm on CoLA, about six times the seed
standard deviation. We build all caches before any arm runs. Any reproduction
that skips this will shift by several points.

\paragraph{The estimator matters as much as the method.} \citet{mantri2026glot}
fix seed $42$ and report the best $\tau$ per task on the split they also report,
so every published number is one draw maximised over a search. We report
$15$-seed means. On CoLA the choice of estimator alone moves the same runs by
nearly $5$ MCC (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Matching their estimator exactly, we
reach $50.52$ MCC on CoLA against a published $47.49$ and $59.57$ on RTE against
$59.21$; STS-B stays $0.87$ short and we could not explain it. Every comparison
in this paper is against our own baseline under identical conditions, never
against a published number.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage A --- the hyperbolic graph: positive on CoLA, null elsewhere}
\label{sec:stageA}

Stage A is nominally ahead of the baseline in five of the seven settings we ran
it in: $+0.70$ MCC on BERT CoLA, $+0.03$ Spearman on BERT STS-B, $+0.04$ F1 on
MRPC, $+0.02$ accuracy on RTE and $+0.04$ Spearman on the decoder, against
$-0.23$ and $-0.09$ on RoBERTa. It is also ahead of the baseline at all four
distractor ratios of the noise diagnostic (Appendix~\ref{app:stress}). At
$n=15$ it reached significance in none of them --- the strongest was CoLA, at
$t=2.37$ with a sign-test $p$ of $0.118$, which fails our both-tests rule.

\paragraph{So we powered it and it separated.} CoLA was the cheapest setting in
which to settle this, and we committed in advance to $n=50$ and to reporting
whatever came back. Over $50$ paired seeds Stage A gives $\bar{d}=+0.834$ MCC
($46.11 \pm 1.54$ against $45.27 \pm 1.68$), $t(49)=3.57$, $p=0.0008$, sign test
$p=0.0153$, $34/16$ seeds positive --- significant on both tests. Because this
is a two-arm re-test of a single pre-specified hypothesis rather than a nine-arm
sweep, the correction is over one comparison, and it survives. The $35$ newly
run seeds agree with the $15$ carried over from the wide campaign ($+0.892$
against $+0.700$), so the effect is not an artifact of the seeds that suggested
it.

\textbf{Verdict: positive on CoLA, null everywhere else.} This is the one place
where more seeds converted a trend into a result, and it is worth being precise
about how little that buys: CoLA is one setting of eight, the effect is $0.83$
MCC, and Stage A remains flat or slightly negative on STS-B, MRPC, RTE, both
RoBERTa tasks and the decoder. We claim Stage A helps on CoLA; we do not claim
the Poincar\'e graph helps in general, and a sign test over the other seven
settings gives $p=0.45$.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage B --- the hyperbolic readout: harmful, and this is the firmest result in the paper}
\label{sec:stageB}

Every arm containing Stage B loses $0.75$--$1.24$ Spearman on BERT STS-B, with
$0$ or $1$ of $15$ seeds positive. B alone gives $t=-17.00$ with $0/15$ seeds
and a sign-test $p$ of $6\times10^{-5}$, the smallest value $15$ paired seeds
can produce. All four B-containing arms survive Bonferroni. The result repeats
on a decoder backbone, TinyLlama, where AB costs $3.56$ Spearman and ABC costs
$4.51$, both $0/15$ and both Bonferroni-significant.

\paragraph{B alone has now been run on a decoder --- but the comparison is
spliced, and we grade it accordingly.} The previous revision claimed a decoder
replication of Stage B without ever having run B by itself on a decoder. B has
now been run, and it is negative: on TinyLlama STS-B, Stage B alone scores
$78.78 \pm 1.05$ against a baseline of $79.95 \pm 0.49$, a difference of
$1.163$ Spearman ($t=-3.66$, $p=0.0026$, sign $p=0.0074$, $2/13$).

\emph{That difference is not admissible under our own rule.} B and C were run on
$31$ August as a separate two-arm campaign, four days after the six-arm campaign
that holds the decoder baseline, and that campaign contains no baseline of its
own. The delta above therefore subtracts a cell from a different campaign ---
exactly the splice Appendix~\ref{app:determinism} shows is unsafe and
Appendix~\ref{app:audit} records as this project's most consequential error.
It is in one respect worse than the case that taught us the rule: no arm was run
in \emph{both} decoder campaigns, so the drift between them cannot be estimated
at all. On CoLA the same drift was $+0.334$ with $\mathrm{sd}\ 1.472$, against
an effect here of $-1.163$.

We therefore report B and C on the decoder as \emph{absolute} scores
(Table~\ref{tab:absolute}), where no cross-campaign arithmetic is involved, and
mark their entries in Table~\ref{tab:verdict} as spliced. The direction is
consistent with the four encoder settings and with BC in the same column, and we
read it as corroboration rather than as a fifth significant result. Stage B is
negative in all five settings that have the power to measure it; it is
\emph{cleanly} negative in four. Closing this properly costs about four
GPU-hours --- fifteen baseline seeds inside the B/C campaign --- and is the
cheapest outstanding experiment in the paper.

It also repeats on a \emph{second encoder}, and the agreement is close enough to
be worth stating numerically. On RoBERTa STS-B, B alone costs $1.264$ Spearman
($t=-15.76$, $0/15$) against $1.065$ on BERT ($t=-17.00$, $0/15$), and AB costs
$0.795$ ($t=-5.77$, $1/14$) against $0.747$ ($t=-5.66$, $1/14$). Both survive
Bonferroni on both backbones. Stage B alone is negative in all four encoder
settings we can measure it in, and $15$ of the $16$ B-containing cells across
those settings are negative.

\paragraph{There is a mechanism, not just a mean.} On the decoder the seed
standard deviation rises in step with the number of hyperbolic parts:
$0.49 \rightarrow 0.95 \rightarrow 1.87 \rightarrow 3.58$ for baseline, BC, AB
and ABC, a $7.3\times$ spread. The gyro-midpoint behaves like a variance
amplifier rather than a useful prior. It is also the stage whose theoretical
motivation is weakest here: pooling averages tokens the graph has already
smoothed, and the Einstein midpoint's curvature dependence pulls hardest exactly
on the large-norm tokens that Appendix~\ref{app:calib} shows are outliers.

\paragraph{One task disagrees, and we state it rather than bury it.} On RTE all
four B-containing arms are \emph{positive} ($+0.79$ to $+1.85$ accuracy). None
is significant, and none could be: RTE has $277$ evaluation examples, a seed
standard deviation of $3.36$, and a minimum detectable effect of $2.06$ points
--- larger than any effect reported in this paper. We read RTE as uninformative
rather than as contrary evidence, but it does mean the honest claim is ``B is
harmful on the tasks that can measure it''.

\textbf{Verdict: harmful. The one claim here that has replicated rather than
merely extended.} The single remaining way it could be an artifact is scale ---
every task we ran has at most $8{,}551$ training examples, and variance
amplification is a quantity more data suppresses. SST-2 would settle it and is
not scheduled.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage C --- hyperbolic message passing: the only stage that ever helps, and we cannot say why}
\label{sec:stageC}

Stage C reaches $+1.42$ MCC on BERT CoLA with $13/15$ seeds positive
($t=4.11$, sign $p=0.0074$) --- significant on both tests, though just above
that campaign's Bonferroni line of $0.0063$. On BERT STS-B, A${+}$C is the best
of the nine arms ($+0.24$) and C is second ($+0.20$). Every arm with the
hyperbolic GNN and \emph{without} the readout is positive on both tasks.

RoBERTa agrees in direction and adds nothing in strength. C is the best arm on
RoBERTa CoLA ($+0.64$, $8/7$) and the best \emph{hyperbolic} arm on RoBERTa
STS-B ($+0.29$, $10/5$), and on the decoder C alone is the highest absolute
score of the nine arms in that column ($80.24$ against the baseline's $79.95$,
nominally $+0.29$ --- but that comparison is spliced across two campaigns and
carries the caveat of \S\ref{sec:stageB}). Stage C is
therefore nominally positive in all five settings it has been run in, but
outside BERT CoLA none of them is significant on either test, and a sign test
over the five gives $p=0.0625$, which cannot reach our bar however the runs had
fallen. Five out of five in the same direction is worth reporting; it is not
worth calling a result. Stage C also does not reach MRPC, where every arm
containing B or C is slightly negative ($-0.15$ to $-0.33$, none significant)
and only Stage A and \texttt{no graph} are nominally ahead.

\paragraph{And it does not decompose --- we powered the test and it still does
not.} The search had given C a $3.6\times$ denser graph than the baseline
($q=0.38$ against $0.10$) together with half the depth and half the width, so
the gain mixes geometry with those choices. We ran the $2\times2$ that separates
them, all four cells inside one campaign, at the $65$ seeds our own power
calculation said were needed to resolve the interaction
(Table~\ref{tab:factorial}). The result is that the interaction is not there:

\begin{center}

\midrule

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three things follow. The total is now much stronger than the $n=15$ campaign
suggested: $+1.69$ MCC with $55$ of $65$ seeds positive, significant on both
tests by a wide margin. The \emph{largest} component is no longer the curvature
but the graph-and-architecture configuration ($+0.88$ against $+0.53$), and
neither is significant on both tests. And the interaction --- the term we sized
the run to detect, estimated at $+1.36$ from $15$ seeds --- is $+0.27$ with
$35/30$ seeds positive. The $n=15$ estimate was noise, which is the same lesson
Appendix~\ref{app:determinism} teaches from the other direction.

\textbf{Verdict: real, and less attributable to geometry than any earlier
revision claimed.} The end-to-end effect is solid. But at $65$ seeds the
configuration term is larger than the curvature term, the two do not interact,
and neither separates from zero on both tests. An honest summary is that
replacing the Euclidean GNN with the M\"obius one \emph{together with} the
denser graph and smaller network it was tuned alongside is worth $+1.69$ MCC on
CoLA, and that we cannot show curvature is the active ingredient. That C is
negative on MRPC, where the search chose a $15\times$ \emph{sparser} graph, is
consistent with the configuration mattering at least as much.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How the stages combine does not transfer}
\label{sec:interaction}

Stage B's harm is stable; what happens when the hyperbolic GNN is added on top
of it is not. Adding C to B rescues the arm in two settings and worsens it in
two others:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Setting & B alone & BC & effect of adding C \\
\midrule
BERT STS-B      & $-1.07$ & $-1.24$ & worse \\
RoBERTa STS-B   & $-1.26$ & $-0.26$ & rescues \\
BERT CoLA       & $-1.26$ & $+0.22$ & rescues \\
RoBERTa CoLA    & $-0.37$ & $-2.16$ & much worse \\
TinyLlama STS-B$^{\S}$ & $-1.16$ & $-1.17$ & no effect \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The extremes are not noise: BC and ABC on RoBERTa CoLA lose $2.16$ and $3.31$
MCC and both survive Bonferroni, while the same two arms on RoBERTa STS-B are
flat ($-0.26$, $-0.13$, neither significant). The decoder row, which the B-alone
run added, is a fifth outcome again: there adding C to B does nothing at all
($-1.163 \rightarrow -1.165$). That row, marked $\S$ above, is the weakest of
the five: B comes from the $31$ August campaign and BC from the $27$ August one,
so it compares two arms that were never run together, and we would not rest
anything on it alone (\S\ref{sec:stageB}). So a two-component summary of the
form ``B harms, C offsets it'' is not supported. Stage B's harm is a property of
the readout, reproducible across backbones and tasks; the sign of the
B$\times$C interaction is a property of the particular task and backbone, and we
cannot predict it.

\paragraph{Why the stage-wise design was necessary.} B and C carry opposite
signs. A study that turned ``hyperbolic'' on as one switch would have reported
their average, which is close to zero, and concluded that geometry does not
matter here. It does --- the parts disagree, and only a stage-wise test can see
that. The cross-backbone comparison sharpens this rather than softening it:
because the size and even the sign of the interaction changes with task and
backbone, the average an aggregate switch reports is not a stable quantity
either. Two studies running the same all-or-nothing comparison on two backbones
could report opposite conclusions without either being wrong.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The premise: measured tree-likeness is an artifact}
\label{sec:premise}

Hyperbolic layers are usually justified by measuring Gromov
$\delta$-hyperbolicity and finding it small. On the Euclidean metric ModernBERT
at layer $16$ gives $\delta = 0.0021$, which reads as almost perfectly
tree-like. But that layer has a maximum-to-median token norm ratio of $156$, and
$\delta$ is scale-sensitive: a few very large ``attention sink'' tokens dominate
it and pull it toward zero --- a star graph is a tree. On the angular metric
$\arccos(\cos(\cdot,\cdot))$, which ignores those norms, ModernBERT gives
$0.2127$ and BERT gives $0.2114$: indistinguishable, while the Euclidean numbers
differ by two orders of magnitude. Flan-T5 shows the same pattern with
$357\times$ norm outliers. So measured tree-likeness cannot tell you which
backbone or which component will benefit from curvature. We did not use it to
choose; we applied all three stages everywhere and let the paired tests decide.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Porting the method to a new backbone}
\label{sec:porting}

Equation~\ref{eq:adj} compares a cosine to a fixed number, which only makes
sense if cosine scale is comparable across models. It is not. On RoBERTa the
\emph{tenth}-percentile token cosine is $0.693$, above the largest $\tau$ in the
published grid, so every searched setting yields an essentially complete graph
($0.991$ density; Table~\ref{tab:density}). The useful finding is what happens
next: \textsc{Glot} is unharmed by that dense graph, while the obvious repair ---
switching to a quantile threshold --- costs $1.41$ MCC ($2/13$, $p=0.0074$;
significant uncorrected, not after the Bonferroni correction the now-larger
RoBERTa campaign requires). It also won its tuning stage before finishing last
of the calibration arms over $15$ seeds, so selection actively favoured it. We
therefore withdraw density-matching as a recommendation for anyone porting this
method.

\paragraph{And the graph itself carries no measurable GLUE signal on either
encoder.} Deleting the token graph outright costs $0.29$ MCC on RoBERTa CoLA
($5/10$) and $0.05$ Spearman on RoBERTa STS-B ($7/8$), neither close to
significant. On BERT the same control is if anything mildly \emph{positive}:
$+0.83$ MCC on CoLA ($11/4$, $p=0.118$, second of nine arms), $+0.03$ Spearman,
$+0.03$ F1 and $+0.07$ accuracy, none significant. So at every tuned density we
searched, the edges contribute nothing measurable on GLUE. We deliberately do
not read this as a verdict on \textsc{Glot}: \texttt{no graph} is still a
trained pooler with the same projection and readout, and the graph \emph{does}
beat real bag-of-tokens poolers by $14$--$35$ points on the noise diagnostic.
But it is the bar any geometry claim has to clear first, and Stage C clears it
on BERT STS-B ($+0.16$, $12/3$) and fails it on BERT CoLA ($+0.58$, $8/7$,
$p=1.00$) despite the $+1.42$ against the baseline.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scope, and one confound it creates}
\label{sec:scope}

\citet{mantri2026glot} report ten GLUE tasks \citep{wang2019glue} on six
backbones. We report four (CoLA, STS-B, MRPC, RTE) with the full nine-arm design
on BERT-base \citep{devlin2019bert}, two with the same design on RoBERTa
\citep{liu2019roberta}, one on TinyLlama \citep{zhang2024tinyllama}, plus
ModernBERT \citep{warner2024modernbert} and SmolLM2 \citep{benallal2025smollm2}
in calibration roles only. The four tasks we chose are the four \emph{smallest},
and not by accident: our design costs $495$ runs per task, which is $22$ hours
for CoLA but an estimated $108$ hours for SST-2 and $185$ and $124$ hours for
QQP and MNLI, with $75$\,GB of cached states between the latter two.

That leaves a confound we cannot currently rule out. Every task here has at most
$8{,}551$ training examples, and Stage B's failure mode is variance
amplification --- a quantity that shrinks as data grows. Our Stage B negative
may therefore be a small-data effect rather than a property of the geometry.
SST-2 ($67$k examples, $7.9\times$ CoLA) is the cheapest test that would settle
it, and we did not run it. We do report IMDB and seven MTEB tasks
(Appendix~\ref{app:docmteb}), but only at one seed, only for the graph stage,
and the MTEB rows turn out to evaluate an untrained pooler --- so they carry no
weight here.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hyperbolic layers, and where our three stages come from.} Hyperbolic
space embeds trees with far less distortion than Euclidean space, which
motivated Poincar\'e embeddings of hierarchies \citep{nickel2017poincare} and
their Lorentz-model successor \citep{nickel2018lorentz}. Turning those
embeddings into layers required a way to do linear algebra on a manifold:
\citet{ganea2018hyperbolic} supplied it, deriving M\"obius linear maps,
logistic regression and recurrent cells from gyrovector formalism, and
\citet{shimizu2021hnnpp} later unified the same components with fewer
parameters. Our Stage C's $W \otimes_c h = \exp^c_0(W\log^c_0 h)$ is exactly
that construction. Aggregation over a \emph{neighbourhood} came next, in two
flavours we reuse directly: degree-normalised tangent-space aggregation
\citep{chami2019hgcn,liu2019hgnn} and attention-weighted aggregation
\citep{zhang2019hgat}, which are our HGCN and HGAT variants. Stage B is older
than either. \citet{gulcehre2018hyperbolicattention} re-expressed soft attention
in the Klein model and aggregated with the Einstein midpoint precisely because
the Fr\'echet mean has no closed form; \citet{chamberlain2019scalable} scaled
the same midpoint to production recommendation. In language specifically,
hyperbolic word embeddings \citep{tifrea2018poincareglove} and, recently,
billion-parameter hyperbolic language models \citep{he2025helm} report gains
over Euclidean counterparts. What none of this literature does is vary its
geometric components \emph{independently inside one architecture}: each paper
proposes a full hyperbolic model and compares it to a full Euclidean one. Our
result is that on \textsc{Glot} those components disagree in sign, so the
aggregate comparison every one of these papers runs would have reported their
average.

\paragraph{Measured tree-likeness as a justification.} The standard empirical
warrant for going hyperbolic is a small Gromov $\delta$, computed on the data
with the four-point condition, at a cost of $O(n^{3.69})$ for an exact answer
\citep{fournier2015gromov}. \citet{khrulkov2020hyperbolicimage} made this the
explicit design step --- measure $\delta_{\text{rel}}$ on a dataset's
embeddings, then choose curvature accordingly --- and
\citet{tifrea2018poincareglove} justify hyperbolic word embeddings the same
way. Our measurement in \S\ref{sec:premise} contradicts the premise rather than
the method: $\delta$ on the Euclidean metric is dominated by a handful of tokens
with enormous norms, and such tokens are now a well-documented and
\emph{architectural} feature of transformers --- attention sinks
\citep{xiao2024attentionsinks} and massive activations
\citep{sun2024massiveactivations}, whose values are largely input-independent.
On an angular metric that ignores them, the backbones $\delta$ is used to tell
apart become indistinguishable. The same outlier and anisotropy structure
\citep{ethayarajh2019contextual,gao2019degeneration} is what breaks the
\emph{cosine} threshold when \textsc{Glot} is ported across backbones
(Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). The closest prior scepticism about curvature is
\citet{kochurov2020createdequal}, who find Euclidean models superior on several
graph tasks; they compare whole models on graph benchmarks, whereas we hold one
architecture fixed and vary its parts.

\paragraph{Sentence pooling.} Most sentence encoders reduce token states with an
order-free operator and put their effort into the training objective, as with
siamese fine-tuning \citep{reimers2019sbert} or contrastive learning
\citep{gao2021simcse}. A separate line builds an explicit graph over text and
runs a GNN on it, classically over a corpus-level word--document graph
\citep{yao2019textgcn}. \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot} is the
sentence-level, frozen-backbone instance of that idea, and the pooler we study;
its components --- graph attention \citep{velickovic2018gat} and
jumping-knowledge aggregation across layers \citep{xu2018jk} --- are standard,
and we also test GCN \citep{kipf2017gcn} and GIN \citep{xu2019gin} in its place
(Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}).

\paragraph{Why the protocol is half of this paper.} A long line of work shows
that architectural comparisons in NLP and ML are dominated by search budget and
seed variance rather than by the architecture.
\citet{reimers2017scoredistributions} showed that the seed alone moves sequence
taggers by a full F1 point; \citet{melis2018stateoftheart} and
\citet{lucic2018gans} found that carefully tuned baselines erase claimed
advances in language modelling and in GANs; \citet{musgrave2020realitycheck}
reached the same verdict for metric learning; and
\citet{narang2021modifications} found that most Transformer modifications do not
transfer at all. \citet{dodge2019showyourwork} give the concrete mechanism we
hit twice --- the maximum over $T$ trials is a biased estimate that reorders
methods as $T$ changes --- and equal-budget random search
\citep{bergstra2012randomsearch} is our response to it. Our retracted Stage A
result (Appendix~\ref{app:narrow}) is a textbook instance. What this literature
does not cover, and what Appendix~\ref{app:determinism} adds, is that on GPU the
seed itself may not identify a run, so the variance pairing is supposed to
remove can be irreducible. The strongest objection to our own negative result
also comes from this literature. \citet{qiu2024computebetterspent} show that
structured replacements for dense layers ``significantly underperform'' under
dense-tuned learning rates and that their benefit appears only with
structure-aware initialisation and step sizes. Our search moved the learning
rate per arm and per task (\S\ref{sec:choices}), but we did not implement
Riemannian optimisation or curvature-aware initialisation, so that objection
stands against Stage B and we record it as such
(Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Whether hyperbolic geometry helps graph-based pooling depends entirely on
\emph{which part} receives it, and the parts disagree enough to cancel.

Stage B, the Einstein gyro-midpoint readout, is reliably harmful: negative in
every one of the five settings with the power to measure it, $0$ or $1$ of $15$
seeds positive on STS-B, surviving Bonferroni on \emph{two} encoders and
repeating on a decoder in combination --- and, on a comparison we can only read
as indicative because it spans two campaigns, alone as well --- with a variance
signature that suggests why. Its effect size on STS-B agrees to within $0.2$ Spearman
between BERT and RoBERTa. Stage C, M\"obius message passing, is the part that
helps --- $+1.69$ MCC on CoLA over $65$ seeds, $55/10$, and nominally positive
in all five settings it has been run in --- but the powered factorial that was
meant to credit curvature for that gain instead put the larger share on the
graph and architecture it was tuned alongside ($+0.88$ against $+0.53$), with no
interaction between them and neither term significant. Stage A, the Poincar\'e
graph, separates from the baseline on CoLA at $50$ seeds ($+0.83$, both tests)
and nowhere else. How the parts \emph{combine} is the one thing that did not
survive a second backbone.

Three lessons travel beyond this architecture. The standard reason to go
hyperbolic --- a low measured $\delta$ --- is an artifact of norm outliers, and
on an outlier-free metric the backbones it distinguishes are the same. A
comparison between geometries is only as good as the budget given to its
control: our own retracted result, and the confound we found in our headline
number, both come from arms that were not given the same freedom. And a seed is
not a run: unless deterministic kernels are requested, re-running one
configuration at one seed can move a metric further than the effect being
measured, which silently invalidates any table that splices cells from two
campaigns. We report all three so the next study does not repeat them.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

All confirmation results use the fixed seed set $\{1,\dots,15\}$, shared across
arms; tuning uses one held-out seed. Every run appends a row to a per-campaign
CSV recording the configuration, the realised edge density, the per-epoch
metrics and the wall-clock time. These logs are released unmodified, including
runs that crashed or produced empty graphs, and the analysis scripts behind
every table read those CSVs directly. Caches are built before any arm runs; that
ordering is load-bearing, not an optimisation (\S\ref{sec:choices}). We do
\emph{not} claim bit-level reproducibility: Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}
measures how far a re-run of the same configuration at the same seed can move,
and the answer is far enough to matter. One environment caveat belongs here
rather than in a footnote: the manifold library that supplies the exponential
and logarithmic maps for all three stages was not version-pinned for most of
this project, and the machine that ran the Table~\ref{tab:absolute} extension
uses a different release from the one that produced the BERT campaigns. No
comparison in this paper crosses that boundary --- every paired test is within a
single campaign on a single machine --- but a reproducer starting from our
\texttt{requirements.txt}, which now pins it, may not land on the same absolute
numbers.

\section{Hyperbolic preliminaries}
\label{app:math}

We work in the Poincar\'e ball
$\mathbb{B}^d_c = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : c\|x\|^2 < 1\}$ of curvature $-c$,
with M\"obius addition $\oplus_c$ and the maps
\begin{equation}
\exp^c_0(v) = \tanh\!\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|v\|\big)\frac{v}{\sqrt{c}\,\|v\|},
\qquad
\log^c_0(x) = \operatorname{artanh}\!\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|\big)\frac{x}{\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|},
\end{equation}
and geodesic distance
$d_c(x,y) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{c}}\operatorname{artanh}\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|{-}x \oplus_c y\|\big)$.
With features rescaled to unit mean norm, $c = r_{\text{eff}}^2$.

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

Frozen backbone, $2$ epochs, Adam, weight decay searched, batch size $32$; the
pooler is the only trained component. Confirmation uses seeds $\{1,\dots,15\}$
shared across arms; tuning uses one disjoint held-out seed.

\paragraph{Matched budget.} Trials per arm are $40$ in the BERT wide campaigns
and on RoBERTa, and $10$ on the decoder and in the earlier graph-only campaigns.
Every arm inside a given campaign gets the same number, which is the property
the paired tests actually need. The decoder gets fewer because a TinyLlama run
costs roughly $4\times$ a BERT run; its arms were therefore selected from a
coarser search, so its entries should be read as paired deltas within that
campaign and not compared against the BERT block. The cosine baseline has a
smaller native space, so we widen its grid to keep selection pressure equal.
Equal trials is not equal \emph{coverage}: $40$ trials cover $6.2\%$ of a
$648$-point space but under $0.01\%$ of ABC's $4.98\times10^{7}$-point space.
This asymmetry works against the complex arms, and we note it as a caveat
against our own negative results rather than in support of them.

\paragraph{Part of the grid cannot train, and almost only on one task.} Pooled
over every campaign with a searched learning rate ($2{,}320$ de-duplicated
tuning trials), $24.3\%$ of trials drawn at $\mathrm{lr}=2\times10^{-5}$ score
zero or below, against $3.5\%$ at $2\times10^{-4}$ and $0.1\%$ at
$1\times10^{-3}$. The effect is almost entirely CoLA's: on BERT/CoLA, $81$ of
$121$ trials at that rate ($66.9\%$) collapse to $\mathrm{MCC}=0$ --- the
classifier predicts the majority class --- as do $14$ of $120$ at
$2\times10^{-4}$, so $26\%$ of that task's nominal $40$-trial budget buys
nothing. RoBERTa/CoLA behaves the same way and slightly worse ($93$ of $130$,
$71.5\%$), while MRPC sits at $2.3\%$ and STS-B and RTE at exactly $0\%$ on both
backbones. Every arm draws from the same grid, so no comparison is biased by
this; but the effective search on CoLA is roughly a quarter smaller than the
headline number, and that is the task where our one positive result lives.

\paragraph{Testing.} We report the exact two-sided sign test beside the paired
$t$-test because evaluation-set quantisation makes the parametric standard error
look too small (MRPC accuracy moves in steps of $0.245$ on $408$ examples). Both
tests must agree. Bonferroni is applied over the arms tested against a common
baseline \emph{within} a campaign: $8$ arms on the BERT campaigns, giving
$\alpha = 0.0063$; $10$ on RoBERTa, where the seven hyperbolic arms share a
baseline with the three calibration arms, giving $\alpha = 0.005$; and $8$ on
the decoder once its B and C arms landed, giving $\alpha = 0.00625$ --- though
those two arms are a separate campaign, so that last count is the conservative
reading rather than the one our own rule strictly implies
(Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}). The count
is a property of the campaign, so adding arms to a campaign tightens the
threshold for arms already in it --- which it has now done twice
(Appendix~\ref{app:calib}, \S\ref{sec:stageB}). Minimum detectable effects at
$n=15$, from the median paired standard error actually observed, are $0.225$
(BERT STS-B), $1.153$ (BERT CoLA), $0.317$ (MRPC), $2.064$ (RTE), $1.000$
(RoBERTa CoLA), $0.243$ (RoBERTa STS-B) and $0.503$ (TinyLlama). RTE's figure
exceeds every effect in this paper, so that task can only ever return a null; we
report it, and draw nothing from it.

\section{A seed does not pin a run}
\label{app:determinism}

\texttt{set\_seed} in the released code seeds \texttt{random}, \texttt{numpy},
\texttt{torch} and \texttt{torch.cuda}. It does not call
\texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms}, does not set
\texttt{cudnn.deterministic}, and does not set
\texttt{CUBLAS\_WORKSPACE\_CONFIG}. Scatter-based neighbourhood aggregation uses
atomic adds on CUDA, whose summation order is not fixed, so two runs of the same
configuration at the same seed diverge from the first backward pass and the
divergence is amplified over two epochs of training.

We measured it by accident. The extended Stage C factorial re-runs the baseline
cell itself rather than borrowing it, and its configuration string is
character-for-character identical to the one recorded for the \texttt{baseline}
arm of the main CoLA campaign. Over the $15$ shared seeds:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Statistic of (factorial re-run $-$ original), CoLA MCC & value \\
\midrule
seeds where the two agree exactly & $0$ of $15$ \\
mean difference & $+0.334$ \\
standard deviation of the difference & $1.472$ \\
largest single-seed difference & $3.61$ \\
\midrule
for comparison: the effect this campaign reports for Stage C & $+1.416$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two consequences, one benign and one not.

\paragraph{Benign: within-campaign paired tests are still valid.} Both arms of
every paired test in this paper were run in the same campaign, once each. Re-run
non-determinism is part of the within-pair scatter that the paired $t$ and sign
tests already estimate from the data; it inflates the standard error rather than
biasing the difference. Nothing in
Tables~\ref{tab:absolute}--\ref{tab:verdict} needs to change.

\paragraph{Not benign: pairing recovers less than we claimed, unevenly.} On
BERT STS-B the paired difference for arm B has $\mathrm{sd}=0.243$ against an
independent-samples estimate of $0.489$ --- pairing halves the variance, as
intended. On BERT CoLA the paired difference for arm C has $\mathrm{sd}=2.08$
against an independent-samples estimate of $1.52$: pairing is \emph{worse} than
not pairing, which means the seed explains essentially none of CoLA's variance
and the run-to-run term dominates. Statements of the form ``every comparison is
paired, so the dominant variance component cancels'' are true on STS-B and false
on CoLA.

\paragraph{And: cells may not be spliced across campaigns.} Our own $2\times2$
in Table~\ref{tab:factorial} originally took two cells from the wide campaign
and ran only the two off-diagonal ones. Substituting a re-run of the shared cell
moved the geometry component of Stage C's gain from $+0.40$ to $+0.07$; the
$65$-seed job, which runs all four cells through one script, one code path and
one cache state, puts it at $+0.534$ (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). The three
numbers span an order of magnitude and differ only in how the shared cell was
obtained, which is the whole argument for never splicing.

\label{app:narrow}

Under the graph-only search, Stage A on STS-B gave $\bar{d}=+0.223$,

\section{Notes for reproducers}
\label{app:defects}

Five details of the released code cost us time. The first three do not affect
the conclusions of the original paper; the last two are asymmetries between our
own arms.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep1pt
\item \texttt{jk\_mode} is advertised as $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max},
      \texttt{mean}, \texttt{none}$\}$, the CLI accepts $\{$\texttt{cat},
      \texttt{lstm}, \texttt{max}$\}$, and the pooler implements
      $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max}, \texttt{mean}$\}$. \texttt{lstm} parses
      and then raises at runtime; \texttt{mean} is implemented but unreachable
      from the CLI. The usable space is $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max}$\}$.
\item Thresholding Poincar\'e \emph{distance} at a fixed radius gives an empty
      graph on real features (see \S\ref{sec:method}). The signature is a score
      that is bit-identical across a whole threshold grid. We use a quantile
      threshold everywhere for this reason.
\item The logged edge-density counts self-loops in the numerator but excludes
      the diagonal from the denominator, so an \emph{empty} graph logs
      $1/(n{-}1) \approx 0.10$ at CoLA's typical length rather than $0$. This is
      a reporting defect only --- no training path reads the statistic --- but
      it is a trap for exactly the audit this paper performs. It was corrected
      partway through the project, so the CoLA logs carry the offset and the
      later MRPC logs do not; densities are not comparable across campaigns
      without checking. Any density we quote is re-measured with the standalone
      instrument of Table~\ref{tab:density}, never taken from training
      telemetry.
\item \textbf{Our Stage C layers add self-loops twice.} The graph builder emits
      self-loops, and \texttt{HyperbolicGCNConv}/\texttt{HyperbolicGATConv} then
      call \texttt{add\_self\_loops} without removing existing ones first, so
      every node gets two. PyG's Euclidean \texttt{GATConv}, which the baseline
      uses, removes before adding. Measured on the real BERT/CoLA cache ($105$
      nodes, $587$ edges) the hyperbolic layers see $692$ edges where the
      Euclidean one sees $587$. Forward-pass effect at Stage C's tuned
      configuration: relative output change $2.38\times10^{-2}$ at $q{=}0.38$
      and $1.92\times10^{-2}$ at $q{=}0.10$, against $4.13\times10^{-1}$ for a
      dense-to-sparse graph change and exactly $0$ for the same call twice. It
      is small, but it is real, and because it differs between the two
      hyperbolic cells it does not cancel in the ``geometry alone'' contrast of
      Table~\ref{tab:decomp}.
      \TODO{This was not fixed in time. The $65$-seed factorial has now measured
      the \emph{uncorrected} layer at high precision, which is exactly the
      outcome the previous revision wanted to avoid. The fix is still
      $\approx$1.5 GPU-hours; re-running the two hyperbolic cells with the
      duplicate removed would tell us whether the $+0.534$ geometry term is
      geometry or bookkeeping.}
\item \texttt{HyperbolicGATConv} accepts \texttt{edge\_weight} and never uses
      it, so \texttt{--edge\_weight\_mode} is a no-op whenever the hyperbolic
      GAT is selected. The Euclidean \texttt{GATConv(edge\_dim=1)} does consume

      affected, but the released grid contains a knob that does nothing for half
      the arms.
\end{itemize}

      already run end to end on 29 August: a smoke test trained the pooler on
      MS MARCO for $823$ seconds, wrote a checkpoint, and scored STS13 at
      $63.94$ against $59.57$ for the untrained pooler. The full campaign was
      relaunched on 31 August and is in flight
      (Appendix~\ref{app:docmteb}).
\item \textbf{Four cost figures were wrong and are corrected.} The projected
      cost of the uncovered GLUE tasks was quoted as $170$ hours for SST-2,
      $264$ for QNLI and $38$ and $41$ \emph{days} for QQP and MNLI. Those came
      from a model that scaled linearly from full training-set size and ignored
      the original's \texttt{train[:20000]} cap on QQP/QNLI/MNLI (their B.3),
      which makes exactly those three tasks cheap. Refitted on our four measured
      tasks the figures are $108$, $83$, $185$ and $124$ \emph{hours}. The
      companion cache figure of $149$\,GB for QQP and MNLI was wrong in the
      other direction for two further reasons --- it assumed $2$ bytes per
      activation where the caches are \texttt{float32}, and it omitted the dev
      split, which for QQP is twice the capped training set. Measured against
      real cache directories the correct figure is $75$\,GB.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Stage C's gain is real but not attributable to geometry.} The
      $2\times2$ of Table~\ref{tab:factorial}, run at $n=65$ with all four
      cells in one campaign, decomposes the gain exactly: geometry $+0.534$,
      configuration $+0.880$, interaction $+0.271$. The total is significant on
      both tests ($t=5.91$, $55/10$); not one component is. The configuration
      term is the larger of the two main effects, so the honest reading is that
      the M\"obius layer helps \emph{in combination with} the graph and network
      it was tuned alongside, and that we cannot isolate curvature as the cause.
      Resolving the interaction at its observed size would need roughly
      $1{,}300$ seeds, which we will not run.
\item \textbf{The decoder's B and C arms cannot be tested against the decoder
      baseline.} They were run as a separate two-arm campaign with no baseline
      of its own, so every delta for them splices across campaigns
      (Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}) --- and with no arm shared between the two
      decoder campaigns, the drift is unbounded rather than merely unmeasured.
      Their absolute scores are sound; their differences are not. Fifteen
      baseline seeds inside that campaign, about four GPU-hours, would fix it,
      and this is the cheapest experiment the paper is missing.
\item \textbf{Stage A's positive is one task deep.} It separates on CoLA at
      $n=50$ ($+0.83$, both tests) and in none of the other seven settings. We
      have not tested whether it survives on a second task at the same seed
      budget, and the cheapest such test has not been run.
\item \textbf{A seed does not identify a run} (Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}).
      Within-campaign paired tests are unaffected, but pairing recovers much
      less variance than we claimed on CoLA, and no table may splice cells
      across campaigns. We did not discover this until the audit for this
      revision.
\item \textbf{Our Stage C implementation duplicates self-loops}
      (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}). The forward-pass effect is about $5\%$ of a
      dense-to-sparse graph change, it differs between the two hyperbolic cells
      of the factorial, and it has not yet been corrected and re-run.
\item \textbf{We select the epoch on the split we report}, because GLUE test
      labels are not public. This is a smaller version of the practice we
      criticise in \S\ref{sec:choices}. Splitting the development set into
      selection and reporting halves would remove the objection, and is the
      single most valuable improvement to this protocol.
\item \textbf{One reproduction residual is unexplained.} STS-B stays $0.87$
      short under every estimator we tried; we tested and ruled out the leading
      hypothesis (Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}).
\item \textbf{The scale factorial is underpowered.} At $n=5$ the exact sign test
      cannot fall below $p = 0.0625$, so no contrast in Table~\ref{tab:fix} can
      meet our own bar.
\item \textbf{Non-Euclidean layers may need structure-aware optimisation.} We
      gave every arm the full grid, and the search did move the learning rate
      per arm and per task rather than pinning it to a flat-tuned value. But we
      did not implement per-manifold Riemannian optimisation or curvature-aware
      initialisation. This is the strongest available objection to our Stage B
      negative, and we state it rather than merely acknowledging it.
\item \textbf{Every task we ran is small, and that may explain Stage B.} CoLA,
      STS-B, MRPC and RTE have $8{,}551$, $5{,}749$, $3{,}668$ and $2{,}490$
      training examples. Stage B hurts by amplifying seed variance, which more
      data suppresses, so its harm may not survive at scale --- and RTE, the
      smallest, is the one task where B-containing arms trend positive. We could
      not test this: at $495$ runs per task our design costs an estimated $108$
      hours for SST-2, $83$ for QNLI, $185$ for QQP and $124$ for MNLI, the last
      three following the original's own \texttt{train[:20000]} cap (their
      B.3). A reduced design --- four arms, confirmation seeds only, reusing the
      CoLA-selected configuration --- brings SST-2 to roughly $13$ GPU-hours and
      $26$\,GB of cache, and is the single most valuable experiment this paper
      is missing.
\item \textbf{RTE is underpowered and contributes nothing.} With $277$
      evaluation examples and a seed standard deviation of $3.36$, its minimum
      detectable effect is $2.06$ accuracy points. All seven hyperbolic arms are
      nominally positive there and not one is significant. We include it for
      completeness and base no conclusion on it.
\item \textbf{Coverage.} \citet{mantri2026glot} report ten GLUE tasks on six
      backbones, plus IMDB and MTEB. We report four GLUE tasks with the full
      nine-arm design on BERT, two with the same design on RoBERTa, one on
      TinyLlama, a synthetic noise task, and IMDB plus seven MTEB tasks at a
      single seed for the graph stage only, the latter with an untrained pooler.
      We never ran Mean, Max, \texttt{[CLS]} or AdaPool on GLUE, so
      Table~\ref{tab:absolute} reproduces the \emph{layout} of the original's
      Table 1 but not its content; our rows are \textsc{Glot}'s own components.
\item \textbf{The stages replicate; their interaction does not.} Stage B's harm
      reproduces across two encoders and a decoder at closely matching effect
      sizes, but the sign of the B$\times$C interaction differs between all four
      encoder settings (\S\ref{sec:interaction}), and at the extremes it is
      Bonferroni-significant in both directions. We have no account of why. This
      bounds what a component-wise study of this kind can deliver: individual
      stages generalise, but a two-stage combination has to be measured on the
      backbone and task where it will be used.
\end{itemize}
