\title{Does Hyperbolic Geometry Help Graph-Based\\
Sentence Pooling? A Controlled Stage-wise Study}

\author{Amit Alfasi \And Avraham Ron}

\begin{document}

\maketitle
\lhead{}

\begin{abstract}
Hyperbolic space represents tree-like structure with less distortion than flat
space, and transformer token states are often described as tree-like. That
pairing has motivated hyperbolic versions of many models. We ask whether it
actually helps, on one architecture, under controlled conditions.

Our subject is \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot}, a sentence pooler that
builds a graph over the tokens of a frozen language model, refines it with a
GNN, and aggregates it into a single vector. Geometry enters in exactly three
places --- how the graph is built (\textbf{Stage A}), how states are pooled
(\textbf{Stage B}), how messages combine (\textbf{Stage C}) --- so we replace
each independently and give all eight combinations the same search budget over
fifteen shared seeds.

The effects are conditional on stage, task, backbone, and training regime:
the same substitution can help or hurt. The largest effects are Stage B gains
and losses under contrastive training and zero-shot evaluation: $+10.5$
Spearman on STS12 and $-15.5$ max-AP on TwitterSemEval2015, both $15/15$ seeds
and surviving family-wise correction. Stage B, the readout, is
reliably harmful under supervised fine-tuning --- on two encoders and a decoder,
the effect size replicating to within $0.2$ Spearman --- because it amplifies
seed variance rather than acting as a useful prior; yet trained contrastively
and evaluated zero-shot it is worth $+10.5$ on STS12 and $-15.5$ max-AP on
TwitterSemEval2015. Stage C's previously reported CoLA gain cannot be attributed
to curvature: its hyperbolic layers duplicated graph-builder self-loops. We
therefore report a new, internally matched four-cell factorial as pending rather
than treating the affected result as evidence. Stage A, the graph, separates on
CoLA at fifty seeds and nowhere else.

Two methodological findings travel beyond this architecture. Low
$\delta$-hyperbolicity, the usual justification for going hyperbolic, is an
artifact of a few very large token norms: on an outlier-free metric the
backbones it is used to tell apart are indistinguishable ($0.2127$ against
$0.2114$), while the Euclidean version differs between them by two orders of
magnitude. And a seed does not pin a run --- re-running one configuration at one
seed moves CoLA by up to $3.6$ MCC, more than the effects being measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Turning a language model's token states into one vector is a first step for
most sentence tasks. Simple poolers --- mean, max, \texttt{[CLS]} --- treat
tokens as an unordered bag. A newer idea is to treat pooling as \emph{relations
first, aggregation second}: build a graph over tokens, refine it with a GNN,
then aggregate. \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot} is a strong example,
matching competitive GLUE and MTEB numbers with $20\times$ fewer trained
parameters and a frozen backbone. Our runs support the claim it rests on: on
the authors' own noise test, \textsc{Glot} beats every bag-of-tokens pooler by
$14$--$35$ points at every distractor ratio, in every seed
(Appendix~\ref{app:stress}).

This paper asks what else that design can carry. Hyperbolic space is a natural
candidate for all three of its parts, so we build a hyperbolic version of each
and measure them separately. That separation is the point: two of the three
move in opposite directions, so a single ``hyperbolic'' switch would have
reported their average and found nothing.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{A stage-wise hyperbolic study} (\S\ref{sec:results}). Under an
      equal search budget with $15$ shared seeds, effects differ by stage,
      task, backbone and training regime. We treat the resolved win/loss tally
      as a descriptive appendix summary, not evidence for a universal direction
      (Appendix~\ref{app:results}).
\item \textbf{A seed is not a run} (\S\ref{sec:choices}). Without deterministic
      kernels, re-running one configuration at one seed moves CoLA further than
      any effect this paper reports. This is not a caveat we inherited from
      elsewhere; it changed one of our own numbers by a factor of six.
\item \textbf{The usual motivation does not survive measurement}
      (\S\ref{sec:premise}). Low $\delta$-hyperbolicity is an artifact of a
      handful of very large token norms.
\item \textbf{The choices that changed our answers} (\S\ref{sec:choices}). A
      cache-warming order worth $5$ MCC, a search that reverses arm rankings, a
      threshold that silently produces a complete graph on RoBERTa, and four
      defects in the released code.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What we changed}
\label{sec:method}

Let $x_1,\dots,x_L$ be the token states of a frozen backbone. \textsc{Glot}
joins two tokens when their cosine passes a threshold,
\begin{equation}
A_{ij} = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, \cos(x_i, x_j) > \tau \,\right],
\label{eq:adj}
\end{equation}
runs $K$ graph-attention layers over the result, and aggregates with a learned
readout. Only the pooler trains. Geometry enters in exactly three places, so we
replace each in turn. Appendix~\ref{app:math} gives the Poincar\'e ball, the
maps $\exp^c_0$ and $\log^c_0$, and the two replaced operators in full;
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows where each stage sits.

\paragraph{Stage A --- the graph.} Lift the tokens into the ball and join them
when their geodesic distance falls below the $q$-quantile of all pairwise
distances. Using a quantile rather than a fixed radius fixes the edge count at
$q$, so Stage A and the cosine baseline are compared at equal sparsity.

\paragraph{Stage B --- the readout.} Replace the weighted sum with the Einstein
gyro-midpoint in Klein coordinates, then map back into the tangent space for the
task head. Unlike a plain mean this depends on curvature: as $c \rightarrow 0$
it degenerates to the arithmetic mean.

\paragraph{Stage C --- the message passing.} Replace each GNN layer with a
M\"obius one that lifts to the tangent space to combine neighbours, using
$W \otimes_c h = \exp^c_0(W \log^c_0 h)$ and either degree-based (HGCN) or
attention (HGAT) weights.

\paragraph{Curvature is searched in a scale-free way.} Curvature and feature
scale are not separately identifiable here: geodesic distance depends only on
$\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|$, so doubling the features and quartering $c$ leaves every
distance unchanged. We therefore rescale features to unit mean norm and search
the \emph{effective radius} $r_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{c}\,\mathbb{E}\|x\|$ over
$\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3\}$ rather than $c$ itself. This is not cosmetic: at raw
BERT token norms ($\approx 14.7$) the map $\exp^c_0$ saturates in
\texttt{float32} and any fixed distance threshold below about $9$ returns an
\emph{empty} graph (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}).

We run all eight combinations of A, B and C, plus a \texttt{no graph} control
that deletes the token graph but keeps the trained projection and readout, and
on RoBERTa two threshold-calibration arms (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{How we ran it}
\label{sec:choices}

Every arm is frozen-backbone, $2$ epochs, Adam, batch $32$; only the pooler
trains. Tuning draws $40$ configurations at one held-out seed ($10$ on the
decoder, where a run costs four times as much); confirmation re-runs the winner
on the shared seed set $\{1,\dots,15\}$. We report the exact two-sided sign test
beside the paired $t$-test, call a result significant only when both agree, and
apply a Bonferroni correction across the pre-specified contrasts sharing a
baseline within a campaign; for MTEB, this family is every complete arm-by-task
contrast for a backbone (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

Six choices moved our numbers further than the effects we were trying to
measure; Appendix~\ref{app:choices} documents all six. One of them changes how
the rest of this paper should be read. \textbf{A seed does not pin a run.} The
released code seeds Python, NumPy and Torch but never requests deterministic
kernels, and the scatter reductions under message passing are non-deterministic
on CUDA. Re-running one configuration at one seed moves CoLA by up to $3.61$
MCC, with paired $\mathrm{sd}=1.47$ --- larger than any effect we report
(Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}). Two things follow. Differencing a cell from
one campaign against a cell from another is not sound, so we mark every such
comparison $\S$ and decline to test it. And pairing recovers less than we
claimed: it halves the variance on STS-B and \emph{increases} it on CoLA, where
the run-to-run term dominates the seed.

\subsection{Stage A --- the hyperbolic graph: positive on CoLA, null elsewhere}
\label{sec:stageA}

Stage A is nominally ahead of the baseline in seven of the ten settings we ran
it in (Table~\ref{tab:verdict}), and ahead at all four distractor ratios of the
noise diagnostic (Appendix~\ref{app:stress}), but at $n=15$ it reached
significance in none of them: the strongest was CoLA, at $t=2.37$ with a
sign-test $p$ of $0.118$, which fails our both-tests rule.

\paragraph{So we powered it and it separated.} CoLA was the cheapest setting in
which to settle this, and we committed in advance to $n=50$ and to reporting
whatever came back. Over $50$ paired seeds Stage A gives $\bar{d}=+0.834$ MCC
($46.11 \pm 1.54$ against $45.27 \pm 1.68$), $t(49)=3.57$, $p=0.0008$, sign test
$p=0.0153$, $34/16$ seeds positive --- significant on both tests. Because this
is a two-arm re-test of a single pre-specified hypothesis rather than a nine-arm
sweep, the correction is over one comparison, and it survives. The $35$ newly
run seeds agree with the $15$ carried over from the wide campaign ($+0.892$
against $+0.700$), so the effect is not an artifact of the seeds that suggested
it.

\textbf{Verdict: positive on CoLA, null everywhere else.} This is the one place
where more seeds converted a trend into a result, and it is worth being precise
about how little that buys: CoLA is one setting of ten, the effect is $0.83$
MCC, and Stage A remains flat or slightly negative on STS-B, both MRPC columns,
BERT and RoBERTa RTE and the decoder's STS-B. We claim Stage A helps on CoLA; we
do not claim the Poincar\'e graph helps in general, and a sign test over the
other nine settings gives $p=0.51$.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage B --- the hyperbolic readout: harmful, and this is the firmest result in the paper}
\label{sec:stageB}

Every arm containing Stage B loses $0.75$--$1.24$ Spearman on BERT STS-B, with
$0$ or $1$ of $15$ seeds positive. B alone gives $t=-17.00$ with $0/15$ seeds
and a sign-test $p$ of $6\times10^{-5}$, the smallest value $15$ paired seeds
can produce. All four B-containing arms survive Bonferroni. The result repeats
on a decoder backbone, TinyLlama, where AB costs $3.56$ Spearman and ABC costs
$4.51$, both $0/15$ and both Bonferroni-significant.

It repeats on a \emph{second encoder} at closely matching effect sizes: on
RoBERTa STS-B, B alone costs $1.264$ Spearman ($t=-15.76$, $0/15$) against
$1.065$ on BERT, and AB costs $0.795$ against $0.747$; all four survive
Bonferroni on both backbones. Stage B alone is negative in all four encoder
settings we can measure it in, and $15$ of the $16$ B-containing cells across
those settings are negative. B alone has now also been run on the decoder and is
negative there ($78.78 \pm 1.05$ against a baseline of $79.95 \pm 0.49$) --- but
that campaign holds no baseline of its own, so the difference splices two
campaigns, which our own rule forbids. We mark it $\S$, decline to test it, and
give the full account in Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}; fifteen baseline seeds
inside that campaign, about four GPU-hours, would close it.

\paragraph{There is a mechanism, not just a mean.} On the decoder the seed
standard deviation rises in step with the number of hyperbolic parts:
$0.49 \rightarrow 0.95 \rightarrow 1.87 \rightarrow 3.58$ for baseline, BC, AB
and ABC, a $7.3\times$ spread. The gyro-midpoint behaves like a variance
amplifier rather than a useful prior. It is also the stage whose theoretical
motivation is weakest here: pooling averages tokens the graph has already
smoothed, and the Einstein midpoint's curvature dependence pulls hardest exactly
on the large-norm tokens that Appendix~\ref{app:calib} shows are outliers.

\paragraph{RTE changes direction with the backbone.} On BERT all four
B-containing arms are positive and none is significant or could be --- that
column's minimum detectable effect, $2.06$ points, exceeds every effect in the
block. On RoBERTa, B and AB are significantly \emph{negative}. On the decoder
all four are positive again and BC reaches $+4.33$ with $14/15$ seeds, surviving
Bonferroni --- the only B-containing arm anywhere in our \emph{GLUE} tables that
is significantly better than its baseline, though that column's baseline sits at
chance (Appendix~\ref{app:chance}). On MTEB, by contrast, B and ABC are both
significantly better than baseline on two tasks each
(Appendix~\ref{app:mteb3}).

\paragraph{On zero-shot retrieval the sign is not stable, and this bounds the
claim.} With the pooler contrastively trained on MS MARCO and evaluated
zero-shot (Appendix~\ref{app:mteb3}), Stage B produces the largest effects in
this paper \emph{in both directions}: $+10.46$ Spearman on STS12 and $+3.86$
ndcg on SciFact, both $15/15$ seeds positive, against $-15.54$ max-AP on
TwitterSemEval2015 and $-3.04$ accuracy on EmotionClassification, both $0/15$.
All four survive Bonferroni on a benchmark better powered than CoLA. The
gyro-midpoint is therefore not harmful \emph{as an operator}; it is harmful in
the supervised fine-tuning regime we measured it in, and helpful on some
zero-shot similarity tasks.

\textbf{Verdict: harmful on the fine-tuning tasks that measure it precisely,
across two encoders and a decoder --- and not harmful as an operator.} The claim
we are entitled to is about a readout in a regime, not about the gyro-midpoint.
One confound remains: every GLUE task we ran has at most $8{,}551$ training
examples and variance amplification is a quantity more data suppresses, so the
negative may be a small-data effect. SST-2 would settle it and is scheduled
last, so at present the confound stands (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage C --- hyperbolic message passing: attribution remains unresolved}
\label{sec:stageC}

The original Stage C implementation appended a self-loop to every loop already
emitted by the graph builder, whereas the Euclidean GAT control first removes
such loops. This changes the relative self-weight and confounds every existing
geometry contrast. The affected BERT CoLA result and the $65$-seed factorial are
retained as an implementation record, but are not used to claim a Stage C effect
or curvature attribution.

\paragraph{Corrective design.} The repaired implementation removes existing
self-loops before adding exactly one, matching the Euclidean control. A new
$2\times2$ factorial reruns all four configuration-by-geometry cells within one
seed-major campaign at the pre-specified $65$ seeds. It must not reuse either
Euclidean cell from the affected campaign: measured non-determinism makes
cross-campaign differencing inadmissible. Until this factorial is complete, the
only defensible Stage C conclusion is that its direction and attribution are
unresolved.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How the stages combine does not transfer}
\label{sec:interaction}

Stage B's harm is stable; what happens when the hyperbolic GNN is added on top
of it is not. Adding C to B rescues the arm in two settings, worsens it in two
others, and does nothing in a fifth (Table~\ref{tab:bxc}). The extremes are not
noise: BC and ABC on RoBERTa CoLA lose $2.16$ and $3.31$ MCC and both survive
Bonferroni, while the same two arms on RoBERTa STS-B are flat. So a
two-component summary of the form ``B harms, C offsets it'' is not supported.
Stage B's harm is a property of the readout, reproducible across backbones and
tasks; the sign of the B$\times$C interaction is a property of the particular
task and backbone, and we cannot predict it.

\paragraph{Why the stage-wise design was necessary.} B and C carry opposite
signs, so a study that turned ``hyperbolic'' on as one switch would have
reported their average --- close to zero --- and concluded that geometry does
not matter here. Worse, that average is not a stable quantity: because the sign
of the interaction changes with task and backbone, two studies running the same
all-or-nothing comparison on two backbones could report opposite conclusions
without either being wrong.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The premise: measured tree-likeness is an artifact}
\label{sec:premise}

Hyperbolic layers are usually justified by measuring Gromov
$\delta$-hyperbolicity and finding it small. On the Euclidean metric ModernBERT
at layer $16$ gives $\delta = 0.0021$, which reads as almost perfectly
tree-like. But that layer has a maximum-to-median token norm ratio of $156$, and
$\delta$ is scale-sensitive: a few very large ``attention sink'' tokens dominate
it and pull it toward zero --- a star graph is a tree. On the angular metric
$\arccos(\cos(\cdot,\cdot))$, which ignores those norms, ModernBERT gives
$0.2127$ and BERT gives $0.2114$: indistinguishable, while the Euclidean numbers
differ by two orders of magnitude. Flan-T5 shows the same pattern with
$357\times$ norm outliers. So measured tree-likeness cannot tell you which
backbone or which component will benefit from curvature. We did not use it to
choose; we applied all three stages everywhere and let the paired tests decide.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Porting the method, and the scope of these claims}
\label{sec:porting}

Equation~\ref{eq:adj} compares a cosine to a fixed number, which only makes
sense if cosine scale is comparable across backbones. It is not: on RoBERTa the
\emph{tenth}-percentile token cosine is $0.693$, above the largest $\tau$ in the
published grid, so every searched setting yields an essentially complete graph
($0.991$ density). The useful finding is what happens next. \textsc{Glot} is
unharmed by that dense graph, while the obvious repair --- switching to a
quantile threshold --- \emph{costs} $1.41$ MCC ($2/13$). We therefore withdraw
density-matching as a recommendation for anyone porting this method
(Appendix~\ref{app:calib}).

Deleting the token graph outright, meanwhile, costs nothing measurable on GLUE
on either encoder, and on BERT is mildly positive ($+0.83$ MCC on CoLA, $11/4$).
We do not read that as a verdict on \textsc{Glot} --- \texttt{no graph} is still
a trained pooler, and the graph does beat real bag-of-tokens poolers by
$14$--$35$ points on the noise diagnostic (Appendix~\ref{app:stress}) --- but it
is the bar any geometry claim has to clear first, and Stage C clears it on BERT
STS-B and fails it on BERT CoLA.

\paragraph{Scope.} We report four GLUE tasks with the full nine-arm design on
BERT-base \citep{devlin2019bert}, two on RoBERTa \citep{liu2019roberta}, one on
TinyLlama \citep{zhang2024tinyllama}, a synthetic noise diagnostic, IMDB, and
the original's seven MTEB tasks; \citet{mantri2026glot} report ten GLUE tasks on
six backbones. The four we chose are the four \emph{smallest}, and not by
accident: our design costs $495$ runs per task
(Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Hyperbolic layers descend from Poincar\'e embeddings of hierarchies
\citep{nickel2017poincare,nickel2018lorentz} and from the gyrovector calculus
that turned them into trainable layers
\citep{ganea2018hyperbolic,shimizu2021hnnpp}. Our Stage C is exactly that
construction, and its HGCN and HGAT variants are the two standard neighbourhood
aggregations \citep{chami2019hgcn,zhang2019hgat}. Stage B is older than either:
the Einstein midpoint was adopted for attention precisely because the
Fr\'echet mean has no closed form \citep{gulcehre2018hyperbolicattention}.
\emph{What none of this literature does is vary its geometric components
independently inside one architecture} --- each paper proposes a full hyperbolic
model and compares it against a full Euclidean one. Our result is that on
\textsc{Glot} those components disagree in sign, so the aggregate comparison
every one of them runs would have reported their average.

The usual empirical warrant for going hyperbolic is a small Gromov $\delta$,
measured on the data and used to choose curvature
\citep{khrulkov2020hyperbolicimage,tifrea2018poincareglove}; \S\ref{sec:premise}
contradicts that premise rather than the method, because $\delta$ on raw
activations is dominated by attention sinks and massive activations
\citep{xiao2024attentionsinks,sun2024massiveactivations}.

Our protocol answers a long line of work showing that architectural comparisons
in ML are dominated by search budget and seed variance rather than by the
architecture
\citep{reimers2017scoredistributions,melis2018stateoftheart,lucic2018gans,musgrave2020realitycheck,narang2021modifications},
and in particular by the biased maximum over $T$ trials
\citep{dodge2019showyourwork}. The strongest objection to our own negative
result comes from the same literature: \citet{qiu2024computebetterspent} show
that structured replacements for dense layers underperform under dense-tuned
learning rates. Appendix~\ref{app:related} gives the full discussion, including
where \textsc{Glot} itself sits among sentence poolers.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Hyperbolic substitutions in graph-based pooling have conditional effects. Their
sign changes with the stage, task, backbone and training regime, by margins up
to $+10.5$ Spearman and $-15.5$ max-AP. A descriptive tally of individually
resolved comparisons is heterogeneous and is not evidence that geometry has a
single direction of effect. The useful question is which experimental regime
produces which effect, not whether curvature works in general.

Stage B, the gyro-midpoint readout, is reliably harmful under supervised
fine-tuning --- negative in every setting with the power to measure it, $0$ or
$1$ of $15$ seeds positive on STS-B, surviving Bonferroni on two encoders, with
a variance signature that suggests why --- and yet, trained contrastively and
evaluated zero-shot, it produces the two largest effects in this paper in
opposite directions. Stage C's prior apparent CoLA effect is confounded by a
self-loop asymmetry and awaits a new matched factorial. Stage A separates from
the baseline on CoLA at $50$ seeds and nowhere else. The \texttt{no graph}
control is essential context: it tests graph contribution relative to the
learned projection and readout, not against bag-of-token poolers.

Three lessons travel beyond this architecture. The standard reason to go
hyperbolic --- a low measured $\delta$ --- is an artifact of norm outliers, so
measured tree-likeness cannot tell you where curvature will help. A comparison
between geometries is only as good as the budget given to its control: our own
retracted result and the confound in our headline number both come from arms
that were not given the same freedom. And a seed is not a run: unless
deterministic kernels are requested, re-running one configuration at one seed
can move a metric further than the effect being measured, which silently
invalidates any table that splices cells from two campaigns.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

Every run appends a row to a per-campaign CSV recording the configuration, the
realised edge density, the per-epoch metrics and the wall-clock time. These logs
are released unmodified, including runs that crashed or produced empty graphs,
and the analysis scripts behind every table read those CSVs directly. Caches are built before any arm runs; that
ordering is load-bearing, not an optimisation (\S\ref{sec:choices}). We do
\emph{not} claim bit-level reproducibility: Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}
measures how far a re-run of the same configuration at the same seed can move,
and the answer is far enough to matter. One environment caveat belongs here
rather than in a footnote: the manifold library that supplies the exponential
and logarithmic maps for all three stages was not version-pinned for most of
this project, and the machine that ran the Table~\ref{tab:absolute} extension
uses a different release from the one that produced the BERT campaigns. No
comparison in this paper crosses that boundary --- every paired test is within a
single campaign on a single machine --- but a reproducer starting from our
\texttt{requirements.txt}, which now pins it, may not land on the same absolute
numbers.

\section{Related work in full}
\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Hyperbolic layers, and where our three stages come from.} Hyperbolic
space embeds trees with far less distortion than Euclidean space, which
motivated Poincar\'e embeddings of hierarchies \citep{nickel2017poincare} and
their Lorentz-model successor \citep{nickel2018lorentz}. Turning those
embeddings into layers required a way to do linear algebra on a manifold:
\citet{ganea2018hyperbolic} supplied it, deriving M\"obius linear maps,
logistic regression and recurrent cells from gyrovector formalism, and
\citet{shimizu2021hnnpp} later unified the same components with fewer
parameters. Our Stage C's $W \otimes_c h = \exp^c_0(W\log^c_0 h)$ is exactly
that construction. Aggregation over a \emph{neighbourhood} came next, in two
flavours we reuse directly: degree-normalised tangent-space aggregation
\citep{chami2019hgcn,liu2019hgnn} and attention-weighted aggregation
\citep{zhang2019hgat}, which are our HGCN and HGAT variants. Stage B is older
than either. \citet{gulcehre2018hyperbolicattention} re-expressed soft attention
in the Klein model and aggregated with the Einstein midpoint precisely because
the Fr\'echet mean has no closed form; \citet{chamberlain2019scalable} scaled
the same midpoint to production recommendation. In language specifically,
hyperbolic word embeddings \citep{tifrea2018poincareglove} and, recently,
billion-parameter hyperbolic language models \citep{he2025helm} report gains
over Euclidean counterparts.

\paragraph{Measured tree-likeness as a justification.} The standard empirical
warrant for going hyperbolic is a small Gromov $\delta$, computed on the data
with the four-point condition, at a cost of $O(n^{3.69})$ for an exact answer
\citep{fournier2015gromov}. \citet{khrulkov2020hyperbolicimage} made this the
explicit design step --- measure $\delta_{\text{rel}}$ on a dataset's
embeddings, then choose curvature accordingly --- and
\citet{tifrea2018poincareglove} justify hyperbolic word embeddings the same
way. Our measurement in \S\ref{sec:premise} contradicts the premise rather than
the method: $\delta$ on the Euclidean metric is dominated by a handful of tokens
with enormous norms, and such tokens are now a well-documented and
\emph{architectural} feature of transformers --- attention sinks
\citep{xiao2024attentionsinks} and massive activations
\citep{sun2024massiveactivations}, whose values are largely input-independent.
On an angular metric that ignores them, the backbones $\delta$ is used to tell
apart become indistinguishable. The same outlier and anisotropy structure
\citep{ethayarajh2019contextual,gao2019degeneration} is what breaks the
\emph{cosine} threshold when \textsc{Glot} is ported across backbones
(Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). The closest prior scepticism about curvature is
\citet{kochurov2020createdequal}, who find Euclidean models superior on several
graph tasks; they compare whole models on graph benchmarks, whereas we hold one
architecture fixed and vary its parts.

\paragraph{Sentence pooling.} Most sentence encoders reduce token states with an
order-free operator and put their effort into the training objective, as with
siamese fine-tuning \citep{reimers2019sbert} or contrastive learning
\citep{gao2021simcse}. A separate line builds an explicit graph over text and
runs a GNN on it, classically over a corpus-level word--document graph
\citep{yao2019textgcn}. \textsc{Glot} \citep{mantri2026glot} is the
sentence-level, frozen-backbone instance of that idea, and the pooler we study;
its components --- graph attention \citep{velickovic2018gat} and
jumping-knowledge aggregation across layers \citep{xu2018jk} --- are standard,
and we also test GCN \citep{kipf2017gcn} and GIN \citep{xu2019gin} in its place
(Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}).

\paragraph{Why the protocol is half of this paper.} \citet{reimers2017scoredistributions}
showed that the seed alone moves sequence taggers by a full F1 point;
\citet{melis2018stateoftheart} and \citet{lucic2018gans} found that carefully
tuned baselines erase claimed advances in language modelling and in GANs;
\citet{musgrave2020realitycheck} reached the same verdict for metric learning;
and \citet{narang2021modifications} found that most Transformer modifications do
not transfer at all. \citet{dodge2019showyourwork} give the concrete mechanism
we hit twice --- the maximum over $T$ trials is a biased estimate that reorders
methods as $T$ changes --- and equal-budget random search
\citep{bergstra2012randomsearch} is our response to it. Our retracted Stage A
result (Appendix~\ref{app:narrow}) is a textbook instance. What this literature
does not cover, and what Appendix~\ref{app:determinism} adds, is that on GPU the
seed itself may not identify a run, so the variance pairing is supposed to
remove can be irreducible. \citet{qiu2024computebetterspent} show that
structured replacements for dense layers ``significantly underperform'' under
dense-tuned learning rates and that their benefit appears only with
structure-aware initialisation and step sizes. Our search moved the learning
rate per arm and per task, but we did not implement Riemannian optimisation or
curvature-aware initialisation, so that objection stands against Stage B and we
record it as such (Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

\section{Hyperbolic preliminaries}
\label{app:math}

We work in the Poincar\'e ball
$\mathbb{B}^d_c = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : c\|x\|^2 < 1\}$ of curvature $-c$,
with M\"obius addition $\oplus_c$ and the maps
\begin{equation}
\exp^c_0(v) = \tanh\!\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|v\|\big)\frac{v}{\sqrt{c}\,\|v\|},
\qquad
\log^c_0(x) = \operatorname{artanh}\!\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|\big)\frac{x}{\sqrt{c}\,\|x\|},
\end{equation}
and geodesic distance
$d_c(x,y) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{c}}\operatorname{artanh}\big(\sqrt{c}\,\|{-}x \oplus_c y\|\big)$.
With features rescaled to unit mean norm, $c = r_{\text{eff}}^2$.

Stage A joins tokens when
$A_{ij} = \mathbf{1}[\,d_c(\exp^c_0(\tilde{x}_i), \exp^c_0(\tilde{x}_j)) < \rho_q\,]$,
with $\rho_q$ the $q$-quantile of all pairwise distances. Stage B replaces the
weighted-sum readout with the Einstein midpoint in Klein coordinates,
\begin{equation}
m_K = \frac{\sum_i a_i \gamma_{k_i} k_i}{\sum_i a_i \gamma_{k_i}},
\qquad k = \frac{2x}{1 + c\|x\|^2},
\qquad \gamma_k = (1 - c\|k\|^2)^{-1/2}.
\label{eq:midpoint}
\end{equation}
Stage C replaces each GNN layer with
\begin{equation}
h_i' = \exp^c_0\!\Big(\sigma\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)}
       \alpha_{ij}\, \log^c_0\big(W \otimes_c h_j\big)\Big)\Big),
\end{equation}
with $W \otimes_c h = \exp^c_0(W \log^c_0(h))$ and $\alpha_{ij}$ either
degree-based (HGCN) or attention (HGAT).

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

Frozen backbone, $2$ epochs, Adam, weight decay searched, batch size $32$; the
pooler is the only trained component. Confirmation uses seeds $\{1,\dots,15\}$
shared across arms; tuning uses one disjoint held-out seed.

\paragraph{Three seed budgets, and which is which.} Two contrasts were run at
larger budgets, and say so wherever they appear: the Stage A re-test on CoLA at
$n=50$ (\S\ref{sec:stageA}) and the Stage C factorial at $n=65$
(Appendix~\ref{app:factorial2x2}). Both were commissioned in advance to settle a
specific question, not selected after the fact for looking better at a larger
$n$. Everything in Tables~\ref{tab:absolute}, \ref{tab:verdict}
and~\ref{tab:mteb3} is $n=15$; the diagnostics elsewhere in the appendix use
fewer seeds and state their own $n$.

\paragraph{Matched budget.} Trials per arm are $40$ in the BERT wide campaigns
and on RoBERTa, and $10$ on the decoder and in the earlier graph-only campaigns.
Every arm inside a given campaign gets the same number, which is the property
the paired tests actually need. The decoder gets fewer because a TinyLlama run
costs roughly $4\times$ a BERT run; its arms were therefore selected from a
coarser search, so its entries should be read as paired deltas within that
campaign and not compared against the BERT block. The cosine baseline has a
smaller native space, so we widen its grid to keep selection pressure equal.
Equal trials is not equal \emph{coverage}: $40$ trials cover $6.2\%$ of a
$648$-point space but under $0.01\%$ of ABC's $4.98\times10^{7}$-point space.
This asymmetry works against the complex arms, and we note it as a caveat
against our own negative results rather than in support of them.

\paragraph{Part of the grid cannot train, and almost only on one task.} Pooled
over every campaign with a searched learning rate ($2{,}320$ de-duplicated
tuning trials), $24.3\%$ of trials drawn at $\mathrm{lr}=2\times10^{-5}$ score
zero or below, against $3.5\%$ at $2\times10^{-4}$ and $0.1\%$ at
$1\times10^{-3}$. The effect is almost entirely CoLA's: on BERT/CoLA, $81$ of
$121$ trials at that rate ($66.9\%$) collapse to $\mathrm{MCC}=0$ --- the
classifier predicts the majority class --- as do $14$ of $120$ at
$2\times10^{-4}$, so $26\%$ of that task's nominal $40$-trial budget buys
nothing. RoBERTa/CoLA behaves the same way and slightly worse ($93$ of $130$,
$71.5\%$), while MRPC sits at $2.3\%$ and STS-B and RTE at exactly $0\%$ on both
backbones. Every arm draws from the same grid, so no comparison is biased by
this; but the effective search on CoLA is roughly a quarter smaller than the
headline number, and that is the task where our one positive result lives.

\paragraph{Testing.} We report the exact two-sided sign test beside the paired
$t$-test because evaluation-set quantisation makes the parametric standard error
look too small (MRPC accuracy moves in steps of $0.245$ on $408$ examples). Both
tests must agree. Bonferroni is applied over the arms tested against a common
baseline \emph{within} a campaign: $8$ arms on the BERT campaigns, giving
$\alpha = 0.0063$; $10$ on RoBERTa, where the seven hyperbolic arms share a
baseline with the three calibration arms, giving $\alpha = 0.005$; and $8$ on
the decoder once its B and C arms landed, giving $\alpha = 0.00625$ --- though
those two arms are a separate campaign, so that last count is the conservative
reading rather than the one our own rule strictly implies
(Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}). The count
is a property of the campaign, so adding arms to a campaign tightens the
threshold for arms already in it --- which it has now done twice
(Appendix~\ref{app:calib}, \S\ref{sec:stageB}). Minimum detectable effects at
$n=15$, from the median paired standard error actually observed, are $0.225$
(BERT STS-B), $1.153$ (BERT CoLA), $0.317$ (MRPC), $2.064$ (RTE), $1.000$
(RoBERTa CoLA), $0.243$ (RoBERTa STS-B) and $0.503$ (TinyLlama). RTE's figure
exceeds every effect in this paper, so that task can only ever return a null; we
report it, and draw nothing from it.

\section{The choices that changed the answer}
\label{app:choices}

The first of the six is in \S\ref{sec:choices} and
Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}; the other five are here.

\paragraph{The search space must be the same for every arm.} An earlier
campaign of ours searched only graph knobs and left the learning rate, depth and
width pinned at values picked for a \emph{flat} pooler. That handed the baseline
an advantage and produced a false positive we retract in
Appendix~\ref{app:narrow}: Stage A on STS-B looked like $+0.223$ Spearman with
$15/15$ seeds and $t(14)=6.20$, and collapsed to $+0.028$ once the baseline was
allowed to pick $K=4$. Every BERT and RoBERTa campaign here uses \textsc{Glot}'s
full grid applied identically to all arms: $40$ trials each, $15$ seeds, $495$
runs per task on BERT and $1{,}980$ across the four GLUE tasks. What has to be
equal is the budget \emph{within} a campaign, not the same number everywhere ---
the decoder gave every arm $10$ trials rather than $40$, for cost.

\paragraph{The search really does use that freedom.} The learning rate does not
settle on one value. On STS-B all nine arms chose $2\times10^{-4}$; on CoLA
eight of nine chose $1\times10^{-3}$; MRPC and RTE split. Depth, width and
threshold move too. Arms were therefore not locked to a rate chosen for a flat
pooler, which is the main form of the objection that non-Euclidean layers get
judged under flat-tuned optimisers. Appendix~\ref{app:hp} lists the selected
configurations.

\paragraph{Tuning maxima are inflated, and can reverse a ranking.} With $T$
trials and per-seed noise $\sigma$, the best of $T$ sits above the truth even
for an arm identical to the baseline --- about $1.83$ here on STS-B. On the
RoBERTa campaign the inflation is large enough to flip the order outright: the
quantile correction is first of five at the tuning maximum ($56.77$) and last
of five over $15$ seeds ($53.19$). Reporting tuning maxima would have inverted
our conclusion. We report confirmation means only.

\paragraph{Cache order is worth about $5$ MCC.} The released code caches
backbone states and builds the shuffled loader \emph{before} the classifier is
created. On a cache miss the loader consumes the global RNG; on a hit it does
not. The same seed therefore gives a different initialisation depending on cache
state --- $40.37$ cold against $45.54$ warm on CoLA, about six times the seed
standard deviation. We build all caches before any arm runs. Any reproduction
that skips this will shift by several points.

\paragraph{The estimator matters as much as the method.} \citet{mantri2026glot}
fix seed $42$ and report the best $\tau$ per task on the split they also report,
so every published number is one draw maximised over a search. We report
$15$-seed means. On CoLA the choice of estimator alone moves the same runs by
nearly $5$ MCC (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Matching their estimator exactly, we
reach $50.52$ MCC on CoLA against a published $47.49$ and $59.57$ on RTE against
$59.21$; STS-B stays $0.87$ short and we could not explain it. Every comparison
in this paper is against our own baseline under identical conditions, never
against a published number.

\section{A seed does not pin a run}
\label{app:determinism}

\texttt{set\_seed} in the released code seeds \texttt{random}, \texttt{numpy},
\texttt{torch} and \texttt{torch.cuda}. It does not call
\texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms}, does not set
\texttt{cudnn.deterministic}, and does not set
\texttt{CUBLAS\_WORKSPACE\_CONFIG}. Scatter-based neighbourhood aggregation uses
atomic adds on CUDA, whose summation order is not fixed, so two runs of the same
configuration at the same seed diverge from the first backward pass and the
divergence is amplified over two epochs of training.

We measured it by accident. The extended Stage C factorial re-runs the baseline
cell itself rather than borrowing it, and its configuration string is
character-for-character identical to the one recorded for the \texttt{baseline}
arm of the main CoLA campaign. Over the $15$ shared seeds:

\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Statistic of (factorial re-run $-$ original), CoLA MCC & value \\
\midrule
seeds where the two agree exactly & $0$ of $15$ \\
mean difference & $+0.334$ \\
standard deviation of the difference & $1.472$ \\
largest single-seed difference & $3.61$ \\
\midrule
for comparison: the effect this campaign reports for Stage C & $+1.416$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two consequences, one benign and one not.

\paragraph{Benign: within-campaign paired tests are still valid.} Both arms of
every paired test in this paper were run in the same campaign, once each. Re-run
non-determinism is part of the within-pair scatter that the paired $t$ and sign
tests already estimate from the data; it inflates the standard error rather than
biasing the difference. Nothing in
Tables~\ref{tab:absolute}--\ref{tab:verdict} needs to change.

\paragraph{Not benign: pairing recovers less than we claimed, unevenly.} On
BERT STS-B the paired difference for arm B has $\mathrm{sd}=0.243$ against an
independent-samples estimate of $0.489$ --- pairing halves the variance, as
intended. On BERT CoLA the paired difference for arm C has $\mathrm{sd}=2.08$
against an independent-samples estimate of $1.52$: pairing is \emph{worse} than
not pairing, which means the seed explains essentially none of CoLA's variance
and the run-to-run term dominates. Statements of the form ``every comparison is
paired, so the dominant variance component cancels'' are true on STS-B and false
on CoLA.

\paragraph{And: cells may not be spliced across campaigns.} Our own $2\times2$
in Table~\ref{tab:factorial} originally took two cells from the wide campaign
and ran only the two off-diagonal ones. Substituting a re-run of the shared cell
moved the geometry component of Stage C's gain from $+0.40$ to $+0.07$; the
$65$-seed job, which runs all four cells through one script, one code path and
one cache state, puts it at $+0.534$ (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). The three
numbers span an order of magnitude and differ only in how the shared cell was
obtained, which is the whole argument for never splicing.

propose to run it, and we do not claim the interaction is zero --- only that it
is not resolvable at any budget this project can reach.

\section{The decoder's RTE baseline is at chance}
\label{app:chance}

The decoder RTE column contains the only B-containing arm in this paper that is
significantly \emph{better} than its baseline, so it deserves a check the other
columns do not need: is the baseline actually learning the task?

RTE's development set has $277$ examples and a majority-class rate of
$52.708\%$ ($146/277$). A model that always predicted the majority label would
score exactly that. Two reference quantities follow. A model guessing
independently per example has a seed-to-seed standard deviation of
$\sqrt{p(1-p)/n} = 3.004$ accuracy points; a model that has learned something
stable should sit well below that.

Three consequences, and they do not all point the same way.

\paragraph{The $+4.33$ is real, and it is not a small-margin artifact.} AC and

$4.5$ points at $p \approx 10^{-8}$, and their seed scatter is roughly half

\paragraph{But the comparison is not the one the rest of the paper makes.}
Everywhere else in this paper the baseline is a working model and the question
is whether curvature improves it. Here the baseline sits at chance
($p=0.83$ against the majority rate), so $+4.33$ measures the distance from

are the ones containing A or B. So the one column where a B-containing arm wins
outright is also a column where Stage C on its own does nothing, which is the
opposite of the GLUE pattern. We report it and offer no mechanism for it.

\section{GNN choice and training recipe}
\label{app:factorial}

\paragraph{GNN backbone.} Over $5$ seeds at the published recipe, CoLA gives GAT
$45.38 \pm 1.19$, GCN $45.14 \pm 1.78$, GIN $44.86 \pm 1.33$; STS-B gives
$82.69 \pm 0.34$, $82.46 \pm 0.27$, $82.47 \pm 0.32$. Spread $0.52$ MCC and
$0.23$ Spearman, inside seed noise, and GAT is the only one of the three that
consumes edge attributes at all.

\paragraph{The STS-B residual is not a recipe mismatch.} The released README
trains differently from the paper's Appendix~B.2 ($3$ epochs,
\texttt{scorer\_hidden} $256$, $\tau = 0.8$ against $2$, $128$, $0.6$). We ran
the $2\times2\times2$ decomposition at seed $42$. Epoch count is the only factor
that moves anything ($82.36 \rightarrow 82.66$); $\tau$ and
\texttt{scorer\_hidden} are inert to within $0.04$. Over $5$ seeds the two
recipes are indistinguishable ($82.69 \pm 0.34$ against $82.69 \pm 0.23$). The
best cell, $82.66$, is still $1.20$ below the published $83.86$, so the residual
stands.

\paragraph{Layer choice overfits.} Layer $8$ beat layer $12$ by $+4.62$ MCC on
CoLA but only $+0.25$ on STS-B, and lost on RTE ($-0.36$) and MRPC ($-0.46$).

\paragraph{A structural prior does not help either.} Since CoLA, MRPC and RTE
are flat for every geometric arm, we also asked whether a \emph{structural}
prior helps where a similarity prior does not: positional edges joining tokens
with $|i-j| \leq w$ (\texttt{POS}), positional edges only
(\texttt{POS\_ONLY}), their union with Stage A (\texttt{A\_POS}), and a $k$-NN
graph (\texttt{KNN}). All four were run on BERT CoLA at the same $15$
confirmation seeds as the rest of the paper: \texttt{POS} $46.10 \pm 1.52$,
\texttt{A\_POS} $46.06 \pm 1.63$, \texttt{KNN} $45.75 \pm 1.44$, and
\texttt{POS\_ONLY} $46.61 \pm 0.85$ at $n=4$ only.

\emph{These are absolute scores, not differences.} That campaign tuned a
baseline but never ran one at the confirmation seeds, so differencing against
the wide campaign's $45.37$ would be the cross-campaign splice of
Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}, and we do not do it. Taken at face value the
four sit within about $0.7$ MCC of each other and of every geometric arm on
that task, which is the same flatness the hyperbolic arms show; we record the
experiment so that ``did you try a non-geometric graph?'' has an answer, and
draw no conclusion from it.

\section{A retracted result of our own}
\label{app:narrow}

Under the graph-only search, Stage A on STS-B gave $\bar{d}=+0.223$,
$t(14)=6.20$, sign $p=6\times10^{-5}$, $15/15$ seeds positive, and replicated on

\paragraph{This benchmark can resolve the effects at issue.} Baseline seed
standard deviations are $0.28$--$1.50$, giving minimum detectable effects of
$0.21$--$1.12$; five of the seven tasks are better powered than BERT CoLA
($1.153$) and all seven are far better powered than RTE ($2.064$). Unlike
Table~\ref{tab:docmteb}, nothing here is excused by sample size.

\paragraph{Two of our headline claims change shape here, and one of them
badly.} Stage B, which is uniformly harmful on GLUE, is the \emph{largest
effect in either direction} on MTEB: $+10.46$ Spearman on STS12 and $+3.86$
ndcg on SciFact, both $15/15$ seeds positive, against $-15.54$ max-AP on
TwitterSemEval2015 and $-3.04$ accuracy on EmotionClassification, both $0/15$.
All four survive the corrected task-by-arm Bonferroni family. Stage A behaves
as it does everywhere else: seven small positives, one of them ($+0.27$ on
EmotionClassification) significant uncorrected, none after correction.

\paragraph{ABC tracks B almost exactly, which says the readout dominates the
stack.} With all six arms now complete, ABC reproduces B's pattern on every one
of the seven tasks and on the same side of zero: $+10.30$ against B's $+10.46$
on STS12, $-15.34$ against $-15.54$ on TwitterSemEval2015, $-3.36$ against
$-3.04$ on EmotionClassification. On SciFact it goes \emph{further} than B,
$+5.57$ against $+3.86$ --- the second-largest positive effect in this paper.
So in this regime adding the hyperbolic graph and message passing on top of the
hyperbolic readout barely moves the outcome: B sets the sign and roughly the
magnitude, and A and C ride along. That is the opposite of the GLUE picture in
\S\ref{sec:interaction}, where adding C to B changed the sign of the result in
four of seven settings, and it is one more instance of the same lesson --- how
the stages combine is a property of the regime, not of the operators.

Stage C alone is nominally negative on five of the seven tasks and significantly
negative on SciFact ($-1.95$, $2/13$) on both tests uncorrected, though not
under the tightened $\alpha=0.00143$.

We do not read this as overturning the GLUE results --- the training regime is
different in kind (contrastive on MS MARCO then zero-shot, against supervised
fine-tuning on the target task), and the same arm can reasonably behave
differently under the two. What it does overturn is the \emph{scope} of the
claim, and \S\ref{sec:stageB} is restated accordingly.

\section{Notes for reproducers}
\label{app:defects}

Five details of the released code cost us time. The first three do not affect
the conclusions of the original paper; the last two are asymmetries between our
own arms.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep1pt
\item \texttt{jk\_mode} is advertised as $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max},
      \texttt{mean}, \texttt{none}$\}$, the CLI accepts $\{$\texttt{cat},
      \texttt{lstm}, \texttt{max}$\}$, and the pooler implements
      $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max}, \texttt{mean}$\}$. \texttt{lstm} parses
      and then raises at runtime; \texttt{mean} is implemented but unreachable
      from the CLI. The usable space is $\{$\texttt{cat}, \texttt{max}$\}$.
\item Thresholding Poincar\'e \emph{distance} at a fixed radius gives an empty
      graph on real features (see \S\ref{sec:method}). The signature is a score
      that is bit-identical across a whole threshold grid. We use a quantile
      threshold everywhere for this reason.
\item The logged edge-density counts self-loops in the numerator but excludes
      the diagonal from the denominator, so an \emph{empty} graph logs
      $1/(n{-}1) \approx 0.10$ at CoLA's typical length rather than $0$. This is
      a reporting defect only --- no training path reads the statistic --- but
      it is a trap for exactly the audit this paper performs. It was corrected
      partway through the project, so the CoLA logs carry the offset and the
      later MRPC logs do not; densities are not comparable across campaigns
      without checking. Any density we quote is re-measured with the standalone
      instrument of Table~\ref{tab:density}, never taken from training
      telemetry.
\item \textbf{Our Stage C layers add self-loops twice.} The graph builder emits
      self-loops, and \texttt{HyperbolicGCNConv}/\texttt{HyperbolicGATConv} then
      call \texttt{add\_self\_loops} without removing existing ones first, so
      every node gets two. PyG's Euclidean \texttt{GATConv}, which the baseline
      uses, removes before adding. Measured on the real BERT/CoLA cache ($105$
      nodes, $587$ edges) the hyperbolic layers see $692$ edges where the
      Euclidean one sees $587$. Forward-pass effect at Stage C's tuned
      configuration: relative output change $2.38\times10^{-2}$ at $q{=}0.38$
      and $1.92\times10^{-2}$ at $q{=}0.10$, against $4.13\times10^{-1}$ for a
      dense-to-sparse graph change and exactly $0$ for the same call twice. It
      is small, but it is real, and because it differs between the two
      hyperbolic cells it does not cancel in the ``geometry alone'' contrast of
      Table~\ref{tab:decomp}.
      \TODO{The layer is now fixed (\texttt{remove\_self\_loops} before
      \texttt{add\_self\_loops}; \texttt{test\_edge\_parity.py} verifies $105$
      against $105$ on the real graph), but the $65$-seed factorial was run
      before the fix and therefore measured the \emph{uncorrected} layer at high
      precision. The corrective factorial is queued rather than run.}
\item \textbf{And the Euclidean control consumes an edge attribute the
      hyperbolic one ignores.} \texttt{HyperbolicGATConv} accepts
      \texttt{edge\_weight} and never uses it, while the Euclidean
      \texttt{GATConv(edge\_dim=1)} does consume it. At the
      \texttt{binary} default every attribute is exactly $1.0$, which looks
      inert --- and an earlier revision of this appendix asserted it was,
      claiming ``no reported comparison is affected''. \emph{That claim was
      wrong.} \texttt{GATConv} forms
      $\mathrm{leakyrelu}(\alpha_{\text{src}} + \alpha_{\text{dst}} +
      \alpha_{\text{edge}})$, and a leaky ReLU is not translation-equivariant,
      so a constant does \emph{not} cancel in the neighbourhood softmax. On the
      real $105$-node CoLA graph, deleting the path changes the layer output by
      $1.36\times10^{-2}$ relative --- $1.2\%$ of a dense-to-sparse graph swap,
      against a noise floor of exactly $0$, and comparable to the self-loop
      defect above. The flag never varies, but the \emph{path} is present in
      every Euclidean arm and absent from every hyperbolic one, so this is not a
      dormant knob: it is a second respect in which every Euclidean-versus-
      hyperbolic contrast in this paper differs by more than curvature. It also
      costs $256$ parameters ($98{,}944$ against $98{,}688$). A
      \texttt{--gat\_edge\_attr} flag now removes it, bringing the Euclidean
      layer to $98{,}688$ parameters against the hyperbolic layer's $98{,}689$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Audit trail for this revision}
\label{app:audit}

Every table was recomputed from the campaign CSVs. Fourteen values changed. They
are listed here rather than corrected silently, because several of them are the
kind of error this paper criticises in others.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep1pt
\item RTE's minimum detectable effect was quoted as $2.7$ accuracy points; from
      the wide campaign's own paired scatter it is $2.064$. It appeared in five
      places. The conclusion drawn from it --- RTE cannot resolve any effect in
      this paper --- is unchanged.
\item The pooled share of tuning trials that fail to train was quoted as
      $19.2\%/2.2\%/0\%$ across the three learning rates; recomputed over all
      $2{,}320$ de-duplicated tuning rows it is $24.3\%/3.5\%/0.1\%$. The
      per-task figures ($81$ of $121$ on BERT/CoLA, $66.9\%$) were correct. The
      RoBERTa/CoLA figure was quoted as $44.1\%$ and is $71.5\%$.
\item Four corrections were arithmetic or labelling: two standard deviations in
      Table~\ref{tab:factorial} ($1.42 \rightarrow 0.90$,
      $2.19 \rightarrow 2.05$); a population-versus-sample standard deviation
      mix in Table~\ref{tab:fix}, now uniformly sample; the decoder baseline
      seed standard deviation ($0.48 \rightarrow 0.494$); and a
      noise-diagnostic row labelled ``A'' when two Stage A variants exist, both
      of which are now shown.
\item The same table omitted \texttt{no graph}, \textsc{Glot} $K{=}0$ and ABC.
      \texttt{no graph} is the best arm at three of four ratios, which reverses
      the paragraph that accompanied the table.
\item ``Stage A is ahead in the noise diagnostic at three of four ratios'' was
      wrong in our own favour: it is ahead of the \emph{baseline} at four of
      four, and behind \texttt{no graph} at three of four.
\item ``On MRPC every hyperbolic arm is slightly negative'' was wrong: Stage A
      is $+0.04$. The claim now names the arms containing B or C.
\item The paper stated that the \texttt{no graph} control had never been run on
      BERT and listed it as an unscheduled experiment. It had been run, on all
      four BERT tasks and on the decoder, and it is mildly positive on BERT ---
      which strengthens rather than weakens the surrounding argument.
\item Table~\ref{tab:docmteb}'s caption claimed its seven tasks were those of
      \citet{mantri2026glot}. They are from that paper's appendix Table 12, not
      its Table 3; only two of seven overlap.
\item The RoBERTa \texttt{published\_tau} arm is significant on both tests
      uncorrected on STS-B ($+0.32$, $t=3.13$, $p=0.035$) and was not marked.
\item ``Jumping-knowledge mode is uniform within a task'' holds on CoLA and

\item The matched-estimator sweep of Table~\ref{tab:repro} was described as

      component of Stage C's gain from $+0.40$ to $+0.07$ --- and the $65$-seed
      run has since moved it again, to $+0.534$ (see below). All three figures
      describe the same contrast and differ only in how the shared cell was
      obtained.

Everything else reproduced exactly: all $8$ arms $\times$ $6$ task-backbone

\section{Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Stage C's gain is real but not attributable to geometry.} The
      $2\times2$ at $n=65$ (Appendix~\ref{app:factorial2x2}) splits the gain
      into geometry, configuration and interaction. The total is significant on
      both tests; \emph{no component is}, and the configuration term is the
      larger of the two main effects. Resolving the interaction at its observed
      size would need roughly $1{,}300$ seeds, which we will not run.
\item \textbf{Stage B's harm is regime-specific, and we found that out late.}
      The same readout that is reliably harmful under fine-tuning produces the
      two largest effects in this paper, in opposite directions, under
      contrastive training with zero-shot evaluation
      (Appendix~\ref{app:mteb3}). We have no account of which task property
      predicts the sign, and with seven tasks we cannot get one. Every Stage B
      statement here is scoped to supervised fine-tuning on the target task.
\item \textbf{The decoder's B and C arms cannot be tested against the decoder
      baseline} (Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}). Their absolute scores are sound;
      their differences splice two campaigns, and with no arm shared between the
      two the drift is unbounded rather than merely unmeasured. Fifteen baseline
      seeds inside that campaign, about four GPU-hours, would fix it, and this
      is the cheapest experiment the paper is missing.
\item \textbf{Stage A's positive is one task deep.} It separates on CoLA at
      $n=50$ ($+0.83$, both tests) and in none of the other seven settings. We
      have not tested whether it survives on a second task at the same seed
      budget, and the cheapest such test has not been run.
\item \textbf{A seed does not identify a run} (Appendix~\ref{app:determinism}).
      Within-campaign paired tests are unaffected, but pairing recovers much
      less variance than we claimed on CoLA, and no table may splice cells
      across campaigns. We did not discover this until the audit for this
      revision.
\item \textbf{Our Stage C control differs from its treatment in two ways that
      are not curvature} (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}). The hyperbolic layers
      duplicated self-loops, and the Euclidean GAT consumes an edge attribute
      the hyperbolic GAT ignores --- which is not inert, because the attribute
      enters before a leaky ReLU. Together they are about $6\%$ of a
      dense-to-sparse graph change, they differ between the two hyperbolic cells
      of the factorial, and the $n{=}65$ factorial was run before either was
      closed. Both are now fixed in code and verified by a forward-pass test,
      but the corrective factorial has not been run, so the $+0.534$ geometry
      term in Table~\ref{tab:decomp} still carries them.
\item \textbf{We select the epoch on the split we report}, because GLUE test
      labels are not public. This is a smaller version of the practice we
      criticise in \S\ref{sec:choices}. Splitting the development set into
      selection and reporting halves would remove the objection, and is the
      single most valuable improvement to this protocol.
\item \textbf{One reproduction residual is unexplained.} STS-B stays $0.87$
      short under every estimator we tried; we tested and ruled out the leading
      hypothesis (Appendix~\ref{app:factorial}).
\item \textbf{The scale factorial is underpowered.} At $n=5$ the exact sign test
      cannot fall below $p = 0.0625$, so no contrast in Table~\ref{tab:fix} can
      meet our own bar.
\item \textbf{Non-Euclidean layers may need structure-aware optimisation.} The
      search moved the learning rate per arm and per task rather than pinning it
      to a flat-tuned value, but we did not implement per-manifold Riemannian
      optimisation or curvature-aware initialisation. This is the strongest
      available objection to our Stage B negative, and we state it rather than
      merely acknowledging it.
\item \textbf{Every task we ran is small, and that may explain Stage B.} CoLA,
      STS-B, MRPC and RTE have $8{,}551$, $5{,}749$, $3{,}668$ and $2{,}490$
      training examples, and Stage B hurts by amplifying seed variance --- which
      more data suppresses. SST-2 has $67{,}349$, $7.9\times$ CoLA, and is the
      cheapest task that can separate the two explanations. It is now queued:
      four arms, confirmation seeds only, replaying the CoLA-selected
      configurations. \emph{That is a weaker design and we will report it as
      one}: because no arm is tuned on SST-2, it can say whether B's harm
      persists at eight times the data but it cannot rank arms, so its numbers
      belong here and not in Table~\ref{tab:absolute}. The reduction is applied
      identically to the baseline and the treatment arms, which is the property
      that makes the contrast fair. Until it lands the confound stands.
\item \textbf{\textsc{Bert} RTE is underpowered and contributes nothing.} Its
      minimum detectable effect of $2.06$ accuracy points exceeds every effect
      in that block, and no arm in it is significant. We include the column for
      completeness and base no conclusion on it. The decoder's RTE column is a
      different matter and carries its own caveat
      (Appendix~\ref{app:chance}).
\item \textbf{Coverage.} We never ran Mean, Max, \texttt{[CLS]} or AdaPool on
      GLUE, so Table~\ref{tab:absolute} reproduces the \emph{layout} of the
      original's Table 1 but not its content; our rows are \textsc{Glot}'s own
      components. What we did run is listed in \S\ref{sec:porting}.
\item \textbf{The stages replicate; their interaction does not.} Stage B's harm
      reproduces across two encoders and a decoder, but the sign of the
      B$\times$C interaction differs between all four encoder settings
      (\S\ref{sec:interaction}) and at the extremes is Bonferroni-significant in
      both directions. We have no account of why. This bounds what a
      component-wise study can deliver: individual stages generalise, but a
      two-stage combination has to be measured where it will be used.
\end{itemize}
